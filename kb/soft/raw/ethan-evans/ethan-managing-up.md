# Managing Up — The Magic Loop, Manager Alliances, and the Veiled Threat

> Distilled from Ethan Evans's "Breakthrough at Senior Manager" course (Day 2). How to convert your manager from "supervisor" into "active sponsor of your promotion," what to do when you have a bad manager, and the chameleon model for adapting to managerial styles.

Your manager is the single most influential person in your career progress. Not your skip-level. Not your VP. Not your peers. Your direct manager.

This is for one simple reason. Promotion processes everywhere — even committee processes — are gated by your manager's advocacy. Your manager either has the explicit authority to promote you, or they have a heavily-weighted vote. Even at companies with elaborate panel processes, your manager's voice carries enormous weight, and their willingness to write a strong document is the entry ticket.

If your manager isn't on your side, you don't get promoted. Period.

So the question becomes: how do you make sure your manager is on your side?

---

## The Magic Loop

I've written about this before — there's a blog post called "The Magic Loop" you can read for more depth. Here's the core.

The Magic Loop is the systematic process by which you build manager trust to the point where your manager becomes an active advocate for your career. There are five stages.

### Stage 1: Do your current job well

You cannot ask your manager to invest in your career if you're not delivering on your current responsibilities. This sounds obvious. It's not.

I've had people come to me and say: "Ethan, my manager isn't supporting my growth." And then I look at their work and they're missing deadlines, they're losing people, their team is unhappy, their projects are slipping.

You don't have a sponsor problem. You have a performance problem. Fix that first.

The first time you ask your manager for help with your career, the only acceptable answer from them — if you're underperforming — is: "The help I need from you is to start delivering."

Do your damn job. That's the floor.

### Stage 2: Ask "How can I help you?"

Once you're delivering, the next move is the one most people skip. You walk into your 1:1 and you ask: **"What do you need from me? How can I help you?"**

That's it.

This is so rare that managers are often startled by it. When was the last time someone on your team asked you that question? Probably never.

Most reports walk in with their own asks. "I need this resource. I need this scope. I need this clarification." Almost no one walks in offering. So when you do, you stand out immediately.

Your manager will tell you what they need. Maybe a project they don't have time for. Maybe a hire they need to lead. Maybe a difficult relationship they need help navigating. Maybe a document they need drafted.

### Stage 3: Do what they asked

Now go do it. Well. Without complaint. With the same energy you'd give your own initiatives.

This is where most people fail. They volunteer in stage 2 and then under-deliver in stage 3. That makes the situation worse than if they hadn't volunteered at all.

If you said you'd help, help. Substantively. Show up.

### Stage 4: Repeat

Do this three or four cycles. Each cycle, you're banking trust. Each cycle, your manager learns that when they tell you something, it gets done.

This takes months. Don't shortcut it. Trust is built with consistency, not single moments.

### Stage 5: Now graduate to proactive moves

Once you have the trust bank, you start making proactive suggestions. Not "what do you need" — but "I noticed we have an opportunity here. I'd like to lead it."

Or even better — you see something, you handle it, you report back. "I noticed X was happening. I took care of it. Here's what I did." Good managers love this. It means you've graduated from someone they manage into someone they trust to operate.

That's the end-state. That's where you want to be.

### Why this works

Your manager has limited time. They are absolutely overwhelmed — probably more than you are. If you become a person who subtracts from their cognitive load instead of adding to it, they will fight for you in rooms you're not in. Not because they like you. Because keeping you and growing you is in their interest.

I'll be blunt about my own thinking. I'm not a cold person. But here's how I think about reports who want my help with their career: if I take another half hour on this person's career, I'm taking it from my family. Why would I do that? Because they're putting in half hour or more to help me. The math has to work.

Make the math work.

## Direct asks: the language of moving things forward

After you've done the trust-building work, you can make direct asks. Most people are too cautious here. They hint. They imply. They never quite say it.

Be direct. The language goes like this:

> "I want to advance my career. I think you and I both want this. Is there something specific I can do — or take on, or contribute — that helps you and also moves me toward the next level?"

This works because:
- You're naming the goal explicitly. No surprises later.
- You're framing it as mutual, not as you asking them for a favor.
- You're inviting them to suggest something specific, which makes the conversation actionable.

Their answer becomes your roadmap. They might say "lead this initiative" or "go fix this team" or "build this relationship with this stakeholder." Whatever they say, that's now your work.

### When to escalate the ask: the veiled threat

Sometimes your manager won't give you a clear path. They keep saying "you're doing great, keep it up." They keep pushing the conversation. The clock keeps ticking.

When that happens, you have to escalate the conversation. The language I used at Amazon when I wanted to move into a new role:

> "My career is naturally important to me. I don't seem to be progressing. To stay, I need to break through this blockage. Can you tell me specifically what I need to do?"

This is a soft ultimatum. Read between the lines: "Either you help me grow, or I'll find growth elsewhere." But it's not aggressive. It's a clear statement of where you are.

If even that doesn't move things, sometimes you escalate further. Toward the end of my Amazon tenure, when I wanted to move from one role to another, I was more direct with my boss:

> "I am leaving this job. The only thing you have a say in is whether I take another job in your team or another team in Amazon or leave Amazon entirely. Pick."

That's a hard ask. I had the standing to make it. I had the trust bank. I had alternatives.

I got the job I wanted next day.

You don't get to make this kind of ask without the trust foundation. But once you have it, sometimes the direct, almost-aggressive ask is what actually moves things. Don't shy away from it when the moment is right.

## Working around a poor manager

Sometimes your manager is the problem. They don't advocate for you. They don't sponsor. They take credit. They block your visibility.

This is real. It happens. And it is one of the most demoralizing situations to be in.

I describe it as a broken leg. You're trying to run with crutches. You can still move forward, but it's harder, slower, and exhausting.

### Step 1: Diagnose — bad manager or style mismatch?

The first question to ask yourself: is this person genuinely a bad manager, or is this a style mismatch?

Ask: "Is anyone else succeeding under this manager?" If the answer is yes — if they have other reports who are doing well, getting promoted, growing — then it's a style issue, not a bad-manager issue. The manager isn't broken; the connection between you two is.

Style mismatches are fixable. The hierarchy says you adapt to them, not vice versa. They set the operating mode; you adapt to it. That's what reports do.

### The chameleon leadership model

This is a useful frame for adapting. Chameleons change color to match their surroundings. As a leader — and a report — your job is similar. You match the operating style of the person you're working with.

If your manager prefers detailed updates, give them detailed updates. If they prefer top-line summaries, give them top-line summaries. If they communicate in writing, write to them. If they communicate in real-time, talk to them in real-time. If they want to be looped in early, loop them in early. If they want to be told only when there's a decision to make, do that.

The more flexible you can be, the more you can work with many different kinds of leaders. The less flexible you are, the smaller the set of bosses you can succeed under.

This isn't being inauthentic. This is operating professionally. You can hold your own personality and values while adapting your communication style.

### Step 2: Try to fix the relationship directly

If it's a style mismatch, ask:

> "How can we improve our working relationship? What can I do to serve you better? What's working, what isn't?"

Most managers don't get this kind of question. Most managers also have weaknesses they're aware of. Sometimes a direct question opens a real conversation that fixes the dynamic.

### Step 3: Shine around them, not through them

If the manager isn't going to advocate for you, you have to make sure your work is visible to people other than them.

- Publish your team's accomplishments broadly. Email lists, Slack channels, internal newsletters.
- Speak up your team members to a wide audience.
- Build skip-level relationships actively (covered in the sponsorship file).
- Find peer feedback providers who will speak well of you in writing.

Don't be loud about it. Just make sure your work has its own visibility, not relying on your manager to carry it.

### Step 4: Document and back up

Build paper trails. Have written feedback that survives a manager change. Develop relationships with multiple people who can speak to your work. Don't be in a position where the only person who can vouch for you is the person who isn't vouching.

Specifically: if your company's promotion process requires six peer feedback providers, build relationships with eight. Have spares. Some will leave, some will get reorged, some will be unavailable when you need them.

### Step 5: Know when to leave

If you've tried steps 1–4 and it's still a broken leg, start planning your exit. Not in panic. Strategically. New job at the same level if necessary, or a move at a slightly lower level if it gets you out of a bad situation.

Caution: don't take your problems to the new job. If part of the issue is your own behavior — not all of it, just part of it — and you don't fix that, you'll repeat the pattern at the next place. I've coached people on their third manager-conflict job. Each time they thought it was the manager. Each time, partly, it was.

Be honest with yourself before you leave. Then leave when you've done the honest work.

### What great manager support looks like

For contrast — when you have a good manager, here's what they do:

- They share credit publicly. Their team's wins are talked about as the team's, not theirs.
- They engage in deep career planning. They actually have opinions about where you should go and why.
- They make introductions. They connect you to mentors, sponsors, peers.
- They take you to meetings so you see the next level firsthand.
- They stand you up in rooms — "Here's why this person is ready for X."
- They give specific feedback, not vague affirmations.

If you have a manager who does these things, you are lucky. Treat them well. Make their life easier. Help them succeed because their success is your success.

If you don't have this, you have to either fix the relationship, work around it, or leave.

## A note on manager transitions

The data is clear: people who change managers during a promotion year tend to get lower ratings than people with the same manager all year.

Why: the new manager doesn't know you. They have no track record with you. They have less credibility advocating for you because they have less data. They have less motivation to fight for you because the relationship is new.

Defenses:

- Work for veterans. They're more likely to stay. They know how the system works.
- Build skip-level relationships so there's continuity above your direct manager.
- Document your work in writing — performance documents, project retrospectives, written wins. These survive a manager change.
- Build a network of peer feedback providers — more than the minimum required.

You can't always avoid a manager change. But you can build resilience to it.

## Putting it together

1. The Magic Loop: deliver → ask how to help → do it → repeat → graduate to proactive moves.
2. Direct asks make things happen. The veiled threat is a tool for when nothing else moves the situation.
3. If you have a bad manager: diagnose first, adapt second, work around third, leave fourth.
4. The chameleon model: adapt your style to the manager's style. They set the terms.
5. Manager transitions are real risk. Build resilience.

The biggest mistake I see is people thinking their manager is supposed to figure out how to support their career. Your manager is supposed to do their own job. Supporting your career is something you have to invite, work for, and earn.

The Magic Loop is how you earn it.

## Action items

- Run the Magic Loop diagnostic on yourself: which stage are you at with your current manager? If you're not at "proactive moves," what's the gap?
- In your next 1:1, ask: "What do you need from me? How can I help you?" Notice if this is a question you've asked recently. Probably not.
- If you've been hinting about your career instead of asking directly: write down the direct ask you'd like to make. Practice it. Use it next time.
- Audit your manager-style match. List three things they value in how you communicate. Are you doing those things, or fighting them?
- If you suspect you have a bad manager: take 24 hours to honestly answer "is anyone else succeeding under them?" Decide your move based on the answer.
