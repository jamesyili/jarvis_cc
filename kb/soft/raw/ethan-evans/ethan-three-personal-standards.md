# The Three Personal Standards of Executive Performance

> Distilled from Ethan Evans's "Breakthrough at Senior Manager" course (Day 1). Voice preserved where possible. Stories embedded with their primary teaching.

When I look at what separates the people who get promoted from the people who stay stuck — at the personal level, what they themselves do — it comes down to three standards. You have to be all three. One isn't enough.

1. **You have to be both scaled AND deep.**
2. **You have to be a change agent.**
3. **You have to be trusted.**

We're going to go through each one. None of these is optional. If you're missing any of them, that is where you're stuck and that is what you have to fix.

---

## Standard 1: Be Both Scaled AND Deep

This is the paradox most senior managers don't see coming. You got to where you are by being personally good at your craft. You're a great engineer, a great PM, a great designer — that's why you got promoted. And then you cross a threshold where your personal ability starts to become your liability, because you use it as a crutch. You run to what you're familiar with. You go solve the technical problem yourself instead of training your team to solve it. You stay in the weeds because the weeds are comfortable.

But here's the trick. You also can't just zoom out. You can't just become a strategy person who has no idea what's actually happening. As you move up, your leaders are going to expect you to know what's going on at the next level of detail BELOW where you operate, AND to hold the strategic picture above. That's both. That's the standard.

I call this the saw model. You alternate. You go high, then you tooth-dive deep, then you come back high. Not consistent altitude — alternating altitude. The CEO of Twitch when I was there was a master at this. He'd be in the strategy meeting at 9am, and at 10am he'd be in a code review with two engineers about a specific bug. That sounds like he was wasting his time. He wasn't. He was tooth-diving — picking one specific thing and going all the way to the bottom — and then he'd pop back up and use what he learned to shape strategy.

### How to scale without losing depth

The mistake people make is they think scaling means stepping back. It doesn't. Stepping back means you don't know what's going on. That's how you get blindsided. Scaling means you set up the **mechanisms** that force the depth into your week without you having to chase it.

Here's the thing about good intentions. If you don't have mechanisms forcing you to do this daily, weekly, and monthly, what happens is you get busy. You mean to check on the team's project status. You mean to dig into that customer escalation. You mean to review the architecture proposal. But you don't, because something more urgent came up. And then a month goes by and you're shocked to discover something is broken.

So you set up the mechanisms. Status reports. Weekly business reviews. Staff meetings with a structure. Skip-level lunches. You're not relying on yourself to remember; you're relying on the calendar.

And inside those mechanisms, you train the leaders under you to feed you the information you need. You explicitly tell them: "Look, I'm expected to know what's going on. Here's what I need from you — problems, schedule overruns, people departing, HR issues, technical blockages. Bring those to me unprompted. Don't wait for me to ask."

Most leaders never have this conversation. They just hope their team will surface things, and then are surprised when they don't.

### Reframe auditing as trust, not distrust

When you do dive deep — and you will — you're going to make people uncomfortable. The team will think: why is my manager suddenly asking detailed questions about this? Are they micromanaging me? Do they not trust me?

Get ahead of this. Tell them: "I'm sorry if you feel like I don't trust you. Rather, I am paid to stay on top of the details. It's not because I don't like you or trust you. It's because that's what my management expects from me."

You're auditing because that's the job. Make that explicit. Otherwise people will assume the worst.

### The metric: how many smart decisions can be made without you in the room?

Here's the test. You scale to the level where smart decisions are being made by your team — well, every day — without you ever being there. That's the goal. You're not trying to be in every meeting. You're trying to make sure that when you're not in the meeting, the right call still gets made.

That requires depth. Your team has to know what good looks like. They have to know what you would say if you were there. That's not micromanagement; that's leverage.

### Steve Jobs on focus

Steve Jobs has this great quote: "People think focus means saying yes to the thing you've got to focus on. It also means saying no to the hundred other good ideas." That's the second piece of scaling. You're not just scaling depth — you're scaling your no.

There's a great article by Brad Porter called "Selective Negligence." Brad reached very high leadership. His thesis is that he ignored a lot of things on purpose. He didn't do them. He didn't respond to them. He let them die. And he was strategic about which ones — the ones that didn't matter, that wouldn't be valued, that weren't in his lane.

Read it. It will free you up.

### The Mike Frisini story

I had a peer at Amazon, Mike Frisini. There was an SVP — a tough one, real heat — who wanted Mike's team to file a regular report. Mike read the request and decided it was nonsense. So he replied — directly — and said: "This report is a waste of my time. I'm making us a ton of money. If you would like me to stop making money to write your report, let me know. I still won't do it."

That is not a tone I'd recommend at every level. But the point stands. Mike was clear about what was valuable, clear about what was a distraction, and willing to push back. That kind of clarity — combined with delivery — is what gets you the latitude.

### Eat that frog

This is the single biggest improvement I made late in my career, and I wish I'd known it earlier. Brian Tracy's *Eat That Frog* — short book, read it on a flight.

The basic idea: your energy declines through the day. So plan your to-do list from hardest to easiest. Or — better — most impactful to least impactful. Do the thing that really matters first.

I tried to earn my entire paycheck by noon every day. By that I mean: by noon, do the one thing that, if I did nothing else for the rest of the day, would justify what they paid me. Then everything else is a bonus.

The trap is the opposite — you start with easy things because they feel productive. Email, small fires, quick wins. Then by 3pm you're tired and the hard thing is still on your list. So you push it to tomorrow. Then next week. I've done shows of hands with crowds of leaders: how many of you can think of something important you've been meaning to do for one week, two weeks, a month or more? Almost every hand goes up.

That's eating the frog wrong.

### Reality check on time

Let me be honest with you about what executive time looks like. My calendar at the VP level: 9am to 6pm packed, five days a week. That's 45 hours of meetings. I ate in meetings. I had two to three hours a week to actually do work.

You will not survive that calendar without ruthless prioritization. You won't. So start practicing now. Send someone else to the meeting and ask for the summary. Reply to the email asking "could this just be an email?" Decline the recurring meeting that has no agenda. Be willing to say no.

### Soft skills are now more critical than hard skills

This is the reframe a lot of high performers miss. At the executive level, your social skills, your ability to build relationships and influence — those will have more impact on your results than your ability to be technically right.

This is frustrating for people who grew up in school being right. Right was a real category there. You got an A. You could prove your answer. As an executive — there is no right answer. There is no clean test. There is influence. There is alignment. There is partnership.

Get used to that.

## Standard 2: Be a Change Agent

Executives are expected to do two things at once that seem in tension:

1. **Handle surprises** — the things you don't control. War. Recession. Pandemic. Return-to-office. A competitor doing something unexpected.
2. **Create the new** — invent things that didn't exist. Lead, not follow.

Peter Drucker said: "The greatest danger in times of turbulence is to act with yesterday's logic." That's the test. The world changes. Are you adapting your logic, or are you running yesterday's playbook?

A change agent invents and solves. Not a passive responder. Active shaper.

### How to actually invent things — systematic innovation

Most people think invention is mysterious. It's not. There's a process.

**Step 1: Build domain knowledge.** You need to study your area systematically to be relatively on the cutting edge. We're unlikely to invent cancer treatment drugs without knowing the body of cancer research. So if you want to innovate in your space, read the literature. Know what's been tried. Know what's failed. Know what's working.

**Step 2: Spend the sacred hour.** This is the one that surprises people.

You don't need a lot of time to invent things. You need a small amount of focused, undistracted time. I call this the sacred hour. One hour per month. That's it.

One hour per month, alone, no laptop open, no phone, just thinking. What do I know that I could put together to invent something? What problems am I noticing? What patterns? Most of my patents — and I have a number of them — were invented in that one hour a month.

The reality? Most leaders spend zero hours per month thinking about innovation. Zero. They're busy. The week fills up. Innovation is something that happens to other people.

You will not innovate if you don't make time. The time is small but it's not optional.

**Step 3: Combine, don't create from nothing.** Most invention is just combining what already exists. Two or three known things plus a problem you've noticed equals a new thing. You don't need to invent the wheel; you need to find an unexpected place to put one.

**Step 4: Test, don't assume.** Don't fall in love with your idea. Test it. Get a small thing in front of users. See what happens. I built my coaching class only after a hundred people said they would sign up. Before that I had a hypothesis; the signups turned it into evidence.

**Step 5: Double down on what works.** This is counterintuitive. The fastest way to grow a business is often not to invent more — it's to look at what's working and do more of that. Test a bunch of small things, find the one with traction, pour gas on it.

### The drone aircraft carrier story

Here's how this actually played out for me. I'll tell you the story so the framework is concrete.

I had two pieces of background knowledge. One: I had read a lot about war history, including World War II aircraft carriers — how they extended the range of fighter planes by carrying them across an ocean and launching them close to where they needed to fly. Two: I had a PhD-adjacent knowledge of robotics, even though it was 30 years out of date. Just the conceptual basics.

The business problem at Amazon: drones can't fly long distance from warehouses. The range is too short. So drone delivery in suburban areas was hard.

In one of those sacred hours, I sat there and thought: is there a way to build a drone aircraft carrier? An aircraft carrier on wheels. A van with an open roof. The drones taxi out, fly to the customer, come back, the van moves slowly through the neighborhood. The drones never have to fly more than a mile.

That was the patent. We filed it. It got issued.

Here's the unexpected part. I assumed it would be useful for drone delivery. What actually happened — the smart TV makers picked it up and adopted the underlying patent for something completely different. The product scaled in a direction I didn't expect.

That patent was a meaningful piece of evidence in my next promotion conversation. Two pieces of background knowledge plus one hour of thinking. That's the math.

### A tool: the Creative Whack Pack

Stanford professor Roger von Oaks built a thing called the Creative Whack Pack. It's a deck of cards or an app — costs about four bucks. Each card is a prompt to break frozen thinking on a problem. "What if you reversed it?" "What would a child do?" "What if it were free?"

If you're stuck on a problem and you want to invent your way out, a Whack Pack card gets you unstuck. I'm not saying it's magic. I'm saying it's a forcing function. Most of us don't break frozen thinking on our own. We need a prompt.

## Standard 3: Be Trusted

This one is the most important. You can be deep, you can be a change agent, but if your leadership doesn't trust you, you don't get promoted. Period.

A political leader I respect once said: "Trust is built with consistency."

That's the whole thing. Consistency. Not brilliance. Not occasional heroics. Consistency.

### Why trust is the gating criterion

VPs cannot audit every decision their senior managers make. They meet with you maybe once a month. The rest of the time, they need to know — when something happens, when a hard call has to be made — that you're going to make a good one.

If they don't believe you're going to make a good call without supervision, they cannot promote you. Why would they? Promoting you means giving you MORE autonomy and a BIGGER scope. They have to believe, baseline, that you're going to make good decisions when no one is watching.

So how do you build that?

**Consistency.** Show up. Deliver. Make the right call. Then do it again. And again. And again.

It's boring. That's the secret. The thing that earns trust is boring.

### The catch-22: you have to take risks but also be trusted

Here's the tension. Trust is built by consistency, but the next-level job often requires you to take bigger risks than you've taken before. So how do you take risks without breaking the consistency?

**Take calculated risks, not gambles.** The military distinction: a risk is when you know the odds AND you've done things to put them in your favor. A gamble is when you don't know the odds; you're just hoping.

Take risks. Never gamble.

If everyone in your peer group plays it safe, some of them will get lucky and stand out anyway. But playing safe doesn't get you promoted on its own. You have to take some swings. The trick is making sure they're calculated.

### The four steps of good decision-making

When you're facing a real decision — the kind that affects whether you get promoted, whether the project ships, whether the team survives — run these four steps. Don't shortcut them.

1. **Get alternatives on the table.** Don't fixate on the first idea. Force at least three options into the conversation. The first idea is rarely the best one; it's just the most available.

2. **Get data.** Hard data when you can get it. Expert consultation when you can't. Experience matters here — talk to someone who's done it before. Don't reinvent the wheel.

3. **Test before committing.** Find the smallest possible version of the decision that you can run. Pilot it. Try it on one team. Run it for a month. Get evidence.

4. **Plan to undo it.** Set checkpoints. "At three months, I'm going to spend a Saturday morning evaluating whether this is working." Have an explicit reversal trigger. The ability to undo a decision is itself a feature; it lets you take more risks because you've pre-baked the off-ramp.

These four steps work for hiring, for org changes, for technology choices, for career moves. Run them.

### Read this book

If you're going to read one book on decision-making, read *Thinking, Fast and Slow* by Daniel Kahneman.

This is my single most-recommended book. I have never had anybody read it and come back saying they learned nothing. It will help you in your professional life and your personal life. It explains why you make the bad decisions you make — the cognitive shortcuts, the biases, the ways your fast brain misleads your slow brain.

If you understand that, you can build mechanisms to slow yourself down on the decisions that matter. That's a real edge.

## Putting the three standards together

Three standards. All three required.

1. **Scaled and deep.** Mechanisms force depth without micromanagement. Eat the frog. Selective negligence on what doesn't matter. Soft skills now matter more than hard ones.

2. **Change agent.** Sacred hour for innovation. Combine known things. Test, don't assume. Double down on what works.

3. **Trusted.** Consistency. Calculated risks, not gambles. Four-step decision-making. Read Kahneman.

If any one of these is missing, that's where you're stuck. Go figure out which one and fix that one first.

The boring part is the trusted standard. People want the romantic story — the brilliant insight, the bold move, the transformation. Trust isn't romantic. It's just showing up and being right, repeatedly, over years.

That's the executive standard. Not flashier. Just steadier and more reliable than the people who didn't get promoted.

## Action items

- Schedule your first sacred hour this week. One hour. No phone. Just thinking.
- Identify one thing you can stop doing — one piece of selective negligence.
- Pick the hardest item on your list for tomorrow morning. Eat that frog before lunch.
- Ask yourself: in the last 90 days, what's a decision I made that I can defend on the four-step framework? What's one I can't?
- Pick one mechanism you don't currently have — weekly business review, structured staff meeting, skip-level lunches — and stand it up.
