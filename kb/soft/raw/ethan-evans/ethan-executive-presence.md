# Executive Presence — What It Is, Why It Matters, How to Build It

> Distilled from Ethan Evans's "Breakthrough at Senior Manager" course (Day 2). What "lacks executive presence" actually means, the audience-specific reads, the biases you have to work around, and the sticky labels that freeze careers.

"You lack executive presence" is one of the most frustrating pieces of feedback you can get. It's vague. It feels unfair. It doesn't tell you what to change. And yet — if leadership is saying it, even if they're saying it in code, you have to address it.

Let me break down what it actually means and how to work on it.

---

## The 60–30–10 model

Executive presence is roughly:

- **60% gravitas** — confidence, command, decisiveness, composure under pressure
- **30% communication** — clarity, storytelling, ability to persuade, reading the room
- **10% appearance** — grooming, dress, the surface stuff

The percentages aren't exact, but the relative weighting is. People talk about appearance because it's easy to talk about. It's the smallest factor. The biggest factor is gravitas — how you hold yourself when things get hard, when you're challenged, when you don't know the answer, when the meeting goes sideways.

Communication is the second factor. Can you tell a story that lands? Can you explain a strategy in three sentences? Can you take a hostile question without flinching? Can you keep the room with you?

Appearance is the smallest factor but it's the easiest to fix. So fix it first. Then work on the bigger ones.

---

## Executive presence is an output, not a test

Here's the reframe that helps. Executive presence is not a thing you have or don't have. It's not a test you pass.

It is the **observable behavior** that emerges from how you actually operate. People look at you in a moment that matters and think one of two things: "I trust this person to handle what's coming next" or "I don't."

That observable signal — that's executive presence. It's the conclusion they draw from watching you. You can't will it into being. You can only train the underlying skills until the conclusion changes.

---

## Audience-specific reads

This is the piece most people miss. Different audiences are reading you for different things.

### What leaders ask when they look at you

Leaders — your skip, their peers, the VP — are asking: **"Are you competent and composed? Can I trust you to handle what I need done?"**

They want to know: if I hand you a problem, will it come back solved? If I send you to talk to a customer, will you make us look good? If I put you in a hostile meeting, will you defuse it or escalate it?

What they're watching for: how you handle stress, how you respond to surprise, how you make decisions when the data is incomplete.

### What peers ask when they look at you

Peers are asking: **"Are you a partner or an adversary? Will you help my agenda or hinder it?"**

They want to know: if I bring you a problem, will you collaborate? If I have to cut your scope to grow mine, will you fight ugly or negotiate well? If I'm absent for a week, can I trust you with my team's interests?

What they're watching for: how you treat their people, how you talk about them when they're not in the room, whether you keep commitments to them.

### What reports ask when they look at you

Your team is asking: **"Will you take care of us? Can I follow you into battle and come out the other side?"**

They want to know: will you fight for resources? Will you protect the team from the political chaos above? Will you back us up when we make honest mistakes? Will you shield us, or sell us out?

What they're watching for: how you handle bad news, how you talk about your own boss, how you treat the most junior person in the room.

### The implication

These three audiences are not looking for the same thing. Gravitas with leaders looks like calm confidence. Gravitas with peers looks like collaborative directness. Gravitas with reports looks like protective steadiness.

The same behavior doesn't read as "presence" to all three. You have to develop a multi-modal version of yourself.

## How to actually develop it

This is where the advice usually gets vague. Let me give you concrete moves.

### 1. Find people who have it. Steal what works.

In your org, identify two or three leaders who clearly have executive presence. Watch them in meetings. What do they do when they're challenged? How do they pause? How do they answer a question they don't know the answer to? How do they push back?

Pick one specific behavior at a time. Try it. See how it lands. Iterate.

You're not trying to imitate them as a personality. You're trying to identify discrete sub-skills you don't have and practice them deliberately.

### 2. Practice in high-stakes moments

The moments where executive presence either shows or doesn't:

- Crises — something just broke, the team's looking at you
- Reorgs — uncertainty, fear, ambiguity
- Hostile questions — someone in the room is challenging you publicly
- Performance reviews — your boss is delivering hard feedback
- Layoffs — you're announcing or absorbing them

Don't avoid these moments. Lean into them. They're the practice ground. When you handle one well, the people in the room notice. When you handle one badly, they notice that too — and you learn what to fix.

### 3. Treat senior people as peer professionals

The biggest tell of someone WITHOUT executive presence is appearing servile to senior people. Tightening up. Saying "yes sir" too often. Laughing too hard at their jokes. Being too eager.

Senior people read this as low-status behavior. They don't necessarily think "this person is being respectful." They think "this person is uncomfortable, they aren't sure of themselves."

Treat senior people as peer professionals. They got there by being good at the job, like you. They have weaknesses and blind spots, like you. They are not gods. Be respectful, not deferential.

This doesn't mean be insubordinate. It means show up like you belong in the room.

### 4. Read these books

A few books worth your time on this:

- **Sylvia Ann Hewlett, *Executive Presence***. She breaks the components down systematically. Useful for self-diagnosis.
- **Stuart Diamond, *Getting More*** — negotiation, but the underlying skill of "keeping the room while not capitulating" is the same skill.
- **Sheryl Sandberg's writing on presence** in different contexts, especially as a woman in male-dominated rooms. The reality of bias is part of presence; her work is honest about it.
- **Anything by Stephen Covey** (*7 Habits*, etc.). The deep operating frame of "seek first to understand" is foundational.

## The bias and culture reality

We need to talk about this honestly because it shapes how presence is read.

The default executive-presence model in American corporate culture was built around a particular archetype: tall, white, male, deeper voice, blue shirt. That's the unspoken reference. It's not fair, it's not right, but it's been the default and it still affects perceptions.

If you don't fit that archetype — if you're a woman, if you're shorter, if you have an accent, if you're young-looking — you have a harder problem. The same behavior reads differently coming from you than coming from the default-archetype person.

### The Margaret Thatcher example

Margaret Thatcher hired a voice coach to deepen her voice. Why? Because the perception research at the time showed that lower voices were rated as more authoritative — and she was a woman trying to lead in a male-dominated political world. She decided the bias was a real obstacle and she'd rather work around it than fight it.

Was that fair? No. Was it effective? Yes.

You'll have to make similar choices. Some biases you fight publicly. Some you work around quietly. Some you ignore. The ratio depends on you, your situation, and what you value.

### Accents

Accents are real obstacles in some contexts, especially American corporate environments, especially at executive altitude. People with strong non-native accents sometimes get coaching to soften them. Some find that empowering; some find it offensive. Both reactions are valid.

If feedback you've gotten suggests an accent is part of the "presence" issue, you have a few options:
1. Work on it (reduce, don't eliminate — eliminating your voice is too high a cost).
2. Compensate with other dimensions — written communication is accent-free, and at executive altitude, written work matters a lot.
3. Choose to work in places where it's a non-issue — international companies, international roles, companies that have actually built around diverse voices.

### Introverts

There's a stereotype that executive presence requires extroversion. It doesn't. Extroverts have one easy version of it (the room-commanding presence). Introverts can have a different equally-effective version — quieter authority, stronger writing, deeper one-on-one impact, decisiveness in moments that matter.

If you're an introvert, don't try to become an extrovert. You'll fail and look uncomfortable. Build the introvert version of presence: clear writing, strong 1:1 work, calm decisiveness, comfortable silence.

## Sticky labels — the impressions that freeze your career

The flip side of executive presence is the labels that get applied to you when presence is missing or miscalibrated. Once these labels stick, they're hard to dislodge.

Here are the common ones I see.

### "Steady and reliable"

This sounds like a compliment. It's a career death sentence at this level.

What it means: you're doing your current job well. People trust you to keep doing it. You'll get 3–5% raises forever. You will not be promoted because no one is imagining you doing the next job. They've categorized you as the person who does THIS job.

What to do: introduce a new note. Take a risk. Lead a change. Innovate. Disrupt the impression. People need to see you doing something they didn't expect before they'll re-categorize you.

### "Nice guy"

Friend zone. You're invited to the parties; you're not promoted to the boardroom. You're seen as agreeable and pleasant, not as someone who drives outcomes.

What to do: take a stand on something that matters. Push back on a bad decision in a meeting. Disagree with a senior person and survive it. The "nice guy" label fades when people see you have edges.

### "Lacks executive presence"

The vaguest one. It can mean almost anything. The sub-causes range from too quiet to too loud, too uncertain to too aggressive, too polished to too rough.

What to do: get specifics. The technique covered in the feedback file: ask for examples, paraphrase back, get to the actual behavior. Don't accept the vague label as the diagnosis. Push for what specifically isn't reading as presence.

### "Doesn't think big"

You're executing well at your current level but not articulating a vision for the next level. You're not changing the company. You're optimizing within the box.

What to do: pick one big thing — one initiative, one strategic shift, one bet — and start carrying it publicly. Speak about it in meetings. Write about it in docs. Make the bet visible. The label changes when people see you operating with a bigger frame.

### "Hard to work with"

This one is fatal at the executive level. You may be delivering, but people don't enjoy your collaboration. At higher levels, where everything is cross-functional and influence-driven, this kills you.

What to do: get specific feedback. Find the people who say this and ask them what specifically they see. The behaviors are usually fixable: cutting people off, dominating meetings, dismissing input, weak conflict resolution. Pick the top one and work on it deliberately.

### Why labels stick

Once a label is applied, the human brain — yours, mine, everyone's — works to confirm it rather than challenge it. People notice the behaviors that fit the label and dismiss the behaviors that don't. To unstick a label, you need overwhelming counter-evidence. Not one good meeting. A pattern, sustained over months, of doing the thing the label said you couldn't.

## Putting it together

Executive presence is the conclusion people draw about whether to trust you with bigger things. It's 60% gravitas, 30% communication, 10% appearance. It reads differently to leaders, peers, and reports.

You build it by:
1. Watching people who have it and stealing specific behaviors
2. Practicing in high-stakes moments instead of avoiding them
3. Treating senior people as peer professionals, not gods
4. Working around — or fighting — the biases that affect how you're read

If you've been given a sticky label — "lacks presence," "nice guy," "steady and reliable" — that label is now your work. You don't get to dismiss it as unfair, even if it is. You either change the behavior or change the perception or both.

The good news: people DO update their views, even if slowly. I have seen many people get unstuck from a label. The technique is consistent counter-evidence over time. Same as building trust. Same as building anything that matters.

---

## Action items

- Identify two or three people in your org with strong executive presence. Pick one specific behavior of theirs and try it this week.
- Ask three people you trust: "What's your honest read on my executive presence? What specifically lands and what doesn't?"
- If you've been given a sticky label, name it. Identify the specific behavior the label points to. Pick one move that disrupts it.
- Check your appearance — the 10% factor. It's the easiest to fix. Get it right first.
- For your next high-stakes moment — a hostile question, a crisis, a hard meeting — go IN, don't avoid. Practice deliberately.
