# Career Strategy — The Long Game, the End Game, and the Cost of Climbing

> Distilled from Ethan Evans's "Breakthrough at Senior Manager" course (PreWork). Why you need an end game before you keep climbing, what the climb actually costs, when to switch companies versus stay, and the regret I want you to avoid.

Most senior managers are climbing the ladder without thinking about where it leads. They see the next level — director, then VP, then SVP — and they assume the climb itself is the goal. They're focused on the move and they don't ask the prior question: where am I going?

This file is about that prior question. It's about the strategic frame that should sit above all the tactical work in the other files.

---

## What is your actual end game?

Forget the next promotion for a minute. Where are you trying to end up?

Are you trying to be:
- A senior executive at a large company?
- A founder?
- A specialist who is the best in the world at one technical thing?
- An investor or board member?
- Someone with the optionality to choose freely after a certain financial point?
- Someone who builds things that matter to a cause you care about?

These are different end games. They require different paths. The next promotion is an interim step toward one of these — but only one of them at a time. Picking the right interim step requires knowing the destination.

### Why the why matters

There's another reason to figure out the end game: your motivation.

You're going to put in years of hard work. You're going to take some bets that don't pan out. You're going to have stretches where things feel slow and the politics feel unfair. The thing that gets you through those stretches is knowing why you're doing the work.

If your why is "more money," fine. Each promotion at this level is worth at least $100K, often several hundred thousand. The financial outcomes are real.

If your why is impact, that's also legitimate.

If your why is influence or autonomy or freedom or some combination, that's fine too. I'm not judging.

But pick a why. Write it down. Your motivation will sustain itself better when you know what you're working for.

### My own why-arc

I'll tell you my own. I went into Amazon expecting to ride a successful career and earn enough to retire. That was the why for many years. It motivated me through some hard stretches.

Then, when I made VP, I had a choice point. I looked at what was next — SVP — and ran the math.

At Amazon at the time, there were about 12 SVPs. They didn't add many. To make SVP I'd be one of the few who'd advance from the roughly 300 VPs. I did an honest self-assessment of the 300. I'd gotten top marks all my life, but looking around at the competition, I was in the middle of the pack. I was harder-working and more capable than some VPs; I was less brilliant and less capable than others.

If about one person a year was making SVP from those 300, was I going to be that one? Honest answer: no. Or at best, it would take me 10 more years of grinding for an outcome that wasn't certain.

I changed why-arcs.

I started mentoring more. I noticed I really enjoyed it. I went onto Twitch and started broadcasting career advice. That morphed into YouTube, then into the work I'm doing now — coaching, teaching, writing. I left Amazon. The career I have now is one I love.

The point: I picked a new why when the old one didn't lead somewhere I wanted. You may need to do the same at some point. Don't keep climbing because the ladder is in front of you. Climb when you've decided the top of THIS ladder is somewhere you want to be.

## What it costs to climb — the unspoken part

I want to be honest with you about what it costs, because most career advice glosses over this. The climb is not free.

### Time

The hardest cost. Time away from family. Time away from hobbies. Time away from things you'd otherwise care about. Senior manager is already a hard job. Director is harder, often the hardest job I had. VP gets a little easier in some ways but the demands extend further into your evenings and weekends.

You won't notice this cost the way it accumulates. Until one day you do, and by then a decade has passed.

### Stress

You're already at a stressful level. It does not get easier. It gets different. The stresses change shape but don't go away. Higher stakes. Bigger blast radius when things go wrong. More people whose careers and incomes depend on your decisions.

### Family cost — what I'll say honestly

I will not be a hypocrite about this. I have been a quite successful executive. I would honestly say I have been a mediocre parent. That's not a thing I'm proud of. I'm just being honest about it.

I made trade-offs there that you should be aware of and not make if you don't want them. The challenge is that companies don't set boundaries for you. Companies are machines. The system is designed to extract as much valuable work from you as it can in exchange for your compensation. That's not evil; that's just what corporate structures do.

If you give the system Friday nights, it's happy. Saturday nights, it's happy. If you work all day Sunday, it's happy. The system will reward the giving. It will never say to you: "Hey, maybe you should stop. Maybe you should be home for dinner."

You will not get that signal from the company. You have to set your own boundaries.

### A story to underline this

A peer of mine got divorced. He went to a support group for divorced executives. Most were Microsoft, slightly older than he was. They laughed at him. They said: "Oh, you're here after your first divorce. You have more coming."

He was shocked. They were casually telling him: yes, you'll probably have another.

I'm not painting that picture to scare you. I'm just being honest. The cost is real. You have to decide how much of yourself you're going to give to the machine.

### What survives

A line worth holding: in 20 years, only your children and parents will remember that you worked late. Your company won't remember. You will probably have changed companies multiple times. The people you worked with will have moved on.

The work matters. The career matters. But the people in your life who love you will outlast the company in your memory.

Do the climb with that math in mind.

## When to switch companies versus stay

A common question: should I stay where I am and try to make director, or move?

Here's my framework.

### When staying is right

- Your manager is highly supportive of your career. They're actively working to grow you. They're willing to put political capital behind you.
- Your company is growing. There's natural opportunity emerging. Roles are opening because the pie is expanding.
- You've built relationships and trust that would take years to rebuild elsewhere.
- The cost of moving (uprooting, learning new culture, restarting trust) outweighs the upside.

### When moving is right

- Your company isn't growing. There's no natural path up because nothing's opening.
- Your manager isn't supportive and you've tried to fix the relationship without success.
- You'd be at a higher level immediately at the new place.
- The pay is materially better and the work is at least as interesting.

### A reframe on staying

I used to believe the "stay loyal to one company" model was the better path. I stayed at Amazon and was promoted there, and I assumed that was the better strategy. I no longer believe that's right, at least not in the current market.

Here's why. When you stay at one company, you have one path up — the path within that company. When you're open to moving, you have access to thousands of director-level openings across the industry.

It's a one-versus-thousand opportunity ratio. If you can find the best of those thousand external opportunities, that's better than the one internal path.

The trick is finding the right one. That's a complex matching problem. So the right answer depends on:
- How much effort you're willing to put into the search
- How portable your skills are
- How much your relationships and reputation are tied to your current employer
- Your personal situation — family, location, life stage

But the default assumption that staying is better is wrong. It's not always better. Often it's worse.

### A real example

A friend of mine was at Amazon. He was offered a VP title and over $1M/year to go to Sears. Sears was a failing company at the time, but they were paying for talent — specifically Amazon-trained talent — to try to revive themselves. He took it.

Sears was a hard environment. He worked there a couple of years. The company didn't really turn around. But here's what happened: when he left Sears, he was a former Sears VP — and that title and Amazon background combined got him a much better VP role at his next company, at a real growth-stage place.

The Sears stint, which would have looked like a mistake from the outside, was actually a strategic move. He used the title and pay to set up the next, better move.

That's a more sophisticated career strategy than "stay at one good company." It's been increasingly common.

### The 2-year rule

If you do switch companies, the rough rule: expect to stay 2 years minimum at whatever level you're hired into. You're rebuilding the trust clock. Internal candidates have a track record. You don't.

Plan for that. Don't switch and expect to be promoted in 6 months. The first 2 years are about establishing yourself in the new place.

## Hard times and low-growth environments

Right now and in similar periods, growth at the big tech companies has slowed. Amazon, Microsoft, Google, Meta — they've done layoffs, they've slowed promotions, some have explicitly frozen them.

I have clients at Google who were told: "We're top-heavy. We have too many leaders. Promotions are frozen for a year." Honest of Google to say it. Also a strong signal — when a company explicitly freezes promotions at your level, you have to consider whether your career is being best served there.

### What to do in low-growth periods

Three options:

1. **Build for when growth resumes.** Use the slow time as muscle-building time. Develop skills, build relationships, accumulate evidence. When the cycle turns, you'll be the obvious choice. This is the patient play.

2. **Move to a high-growth environment.** Smaller companies, faster-moving markets, areas with active investment. Capital One was hiring aggressively a while back. AI labs hire aggressively now. Find the place where the pie is growing.

3. **Reframe the goal.** Maybe the next promotion isn't your real goal. Maybe it's a different kind of impact, a different kind of role. The slowdown might be the prompt to reconsider.

The mistake is panicking. Or staying passive. Or convincing yourself it's about you when it's actually structural.

### A reminder on timeframes

Careers are 30 years. A 12-month slowdown is 3% of that. Even a 24-month slowdown is 7%. That's not a career-defining stretch.

Do not let a one-year slowdown push you into a panicked move that lasts 5 years. The math doesn't favor that.

Push hard within the period. Be tactical. Build during the slow time. Don't over-react.

## My biggest career regret — overworrying

I'll close with my biggest regret, because I want you to avoid it.

I worried too much.

I was always worried about the next promotion. Whether I'd make it. Whether I'd be perceived right. Whether I'd accumulated enough money. Whether I'd be financially independent in time. The worry was constant and it was background noise that ran for years.

In hindsight, all that worry was overkill. Things worked out. They took longer than I wanted in some places, faster in others, but they worked out. I would have been kinder to myself if I'd worried less and trusted the long arc more.

You probably have similar worry running. The fact that you're investing in courses, reading career books, talking to coaches — that's evidence of healthy ambition, but also evidence of worry. Some worry is functional. It causes you to take action. Too much worry is exhausting and doesn't change outcomes.

### What I wish I'd held onto

A few things I'd tell my younger self:

- **You will probably be fine.** If you keep working hard and smart, the career will work itself out. Not on the timeline you want — but it'll work out.
- **A six-month delay isn't a verdict.** Even a year. Stretch the timeframe out. 30-year career means a year is 3%.
- **Take more risks, sooner.** I was too cautious. The risks I did take got me promoted. I should have taken more.
- **Build the network earlier.** I built mine in three years near the end. I wish I'd built it twenty years earlier.
- **Don't sacrifice family for career trajectory.** I made trade-offs there I shouldn't have made.

If you're going to take one piece of advice from this whole file, take this: trust the arc more than I did. Worry less. Build more. Be present in the rest of your life along the way.

## What you're really doing here

You're playing chess. Many moves, many pieces, many ways to win. The game is long. Your specific situation is one position on the board, not the whole game.

A few principles:

- **Know your end game** so you can move your pieces with intention.
- **Pick interim steps** that lead toward the end game. Not just whatever next-level looks attractive.
- **Take calculated risks** because they're the moves that change the game.
- **Build relationships continuously** because they outlast any single position.
- **Be willing to change strategies** when the board changes. I changed my end game when SVP looked unwise. You may too.
- **Worry less, build more.**

That's the whole game in one paragraph.

## Putting it together

1. Pick an end game. Know why you're climbing.
2. The climb has costs. Don't ignore them. Set your own boundaries because the company won't.
3. Stay vs. move depends on growth, manager support, and the quality of alternatives. Default-staying is no longer right.
4. In low-growth periods, build, move, or reframe. Don't panic.
5. My biggest regret is overworrying. Avoid it.
6. You're playing chess. Long game.

You're already winning. You speak English, you're well-paid, you have a successful career. Most of the hard part is behind you. The question is what you do with the position you've built. Pick well. Work hard. Be present in the rest of your life.

## Action items

- Write your end game in one sentence. Where are you trying to end up? What will fulfill you?
- Audit the cost of your current pace. What are you giving up? Is the trade-off the one you want to make?
- Honestly assess: should I stay or should I move? Run the framework. Decide deliberately, not by inertia.
- Identify one thing you've been worrying about that's eaten more cycles than the worry deserves. Stop.
- Identify one thing you've been NOT doing because of worry — a risk you've avoided, a conversation you've delayed, a move you've put off. Do it this month.
