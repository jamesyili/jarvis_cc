# Calculated Risk and Managing Failure — How Real Promotions Get Triggered

> Distilled from Ethan Evans's "Breakthrough at Senior Manager" course (Day 1). Why playing safe doesn't get you promoted, the calculated-risk frameworks I use, the TiVo bet that triggered my own director promotion, and how to recover when something goes badly wrong.

If you're going to get promoted, at some point you're going to need to take a real risk. A bet that, if it works, makes the case for you — and if it doesn't, costs you something real.

This file is about how to take those bets well, and how to recover when one of them goes against you.

---

## The catch-22 of trust and risk

You build trust through consistency. The next-level role requires you to take bigger risks than you've taken before. So how do you do both?

This is the central tension of moving up. The people who only build trust through reliability stay reliable forever. They get the "steady and reliable" sticky label and don't get promoted. The people who only take big risks without the trust foundation get fired when one of the bets fails.

The answer is: take **calculated risks**, not gambles. The military distinction is useful here.

- **Risk** — you know the odds AND you've done things to put them in your favor.
- **Gamble** — you don't know the odds. You're hoping.

Take risks. Never gamble.

The next-level promotions go to the people who took some calculated risks AND mostly got them right AND maintained the consistency on everything else. Not the cautious people. Not the reckless people. The ones in the middle who picked their bets well.

---

## Why playing safe doesn't work

If everyone plays it safe — and your competition for the next role is somewhere between five and fifty other people, depending on the company — some of them will not play safe. Some of them will take swings. And even dumb swings sometimes connect. Which means if you're playing safe and they're not, you're losing the variance battle.

Playing safe also tends to read as "doesn't think big" — the sticky label that quietly shuts down promotion conversations. You can be technically excellent and still not get promoted because nobody saw you do anything bold.

So the question isn't whether to take risks. It's how to take them well.

## The 70% rule

Jeff Bezos popularized this one and it's stuck with me. **Most decisions should be made with about 70% of the information you wish you had.**

Waiting for 90% is too slow. Big companies tend to make good decisions, but make them too slowly. By the time you have 90% of the information, the window has passed, the competitor has moved, the opportunity is gone.

70% is the sweet spot. Enough to have a reasonable view. Not so much that the decision is academic.

The 70% rule is also a way to manage your own perfectionism. If you're the kind of person who wants to be fully informed before any decision, you're going to be too slow at the executive level. Practice making 70% decisions deliberately. Get comfortable with the discomfort.

---

## One-way doors versus two-way doors

This is the other Bezos framework that earns its place. Not all decisions are the same.

- **Two-way doors** — easy to reverse. If you walk through and don't like what's on the other side, you walk back. Most decisions are like this.
- **One-way doors** — hard to reverse. Once you walk through, you're committed. You can't easily go back.

The strategic implication: **move fast through two-way doors, move carefully through one-way doors.**

Examples:
- Pricing decisions: easy to lower a price (two-way). Hard to raise one (one-way). So if you're not sure, start high.
- Hiring decisions: hiring is easier to undo than firing. So make hiring choices with 70%, firing with 90%.
- Public commitments: easy to internally pivot, hard to publicly retract. So commit publicly with 90%.
- Architectural choices: depends entirely on the platform. Some are reversible. Some are foundational.

The mistake people make is treating all decisions as one-way doors. They get cautious about everything. They slow themselves down on reversible bets. The result: they're slow on the easy stuff and not actually any better on the hard stuff.

Sort your decisions before you make them. "Is this one-way or two-way?" If it's two-way, move fast. If it's one-way, slow down.

## The four steps of calculated risk

When you're facing a real bet, run these four:

1. **Get alternatives on the table.** The first idea is rarely the best. Force at least three options into consideration. The act of generating alternatives is itself a sanity check — sometimes you discover the obvious option is actually the wrong one.

2. **Get data.** Hard data when available. Expert consultation when not. Don't reinvent the wheel — find someone who has done something similar, ask them what worked and what didn't.

3. **Test before committing.** Find the smallest version of the bet you can run. Pilot it. Try it on one team for a month. The cost of testing is small; the information from testing is huge.

4. **Plan to undo it.** Set explicit checkpoints. "At three months, I'm going to spend two hours evaluating whether this is working. If it's not, here's the off-ramp." Pre-bake the reversal. This lets you take more risks because you've planned for failure already.

These four steps work for technology bets, hiring decisions, org changes, career moves, and most other significant decisions. Run them.

## The TiVo story — the bet that got me to director

Let me tell you about the specific bet that triggered my own director promotion at Amazon. It illustrates calculated risk well because the dynamics are recognizable.

The setup: I was a senior manager at Amazon, running an early version of what would become Prime Video. The leadership team was discussing whether to do a partnership with TiVo — putting our video service onto their devices.

The room was risk-averse. My business partner said: "I wouldn't do it. The integration is uncertain. It's outside our core focus." The SVP said: "I'm leaving it up to you."

I was alone in the room with people who didn't want to do it. But I could see what most of them couldn't: the engineering cost was actually small. About 7% of our resources. The downside if it failed was modest. The upside if it worked was a foothold on every TiVo device in the country.

I made the call. I said something like: "Unless you order me not to, I'm going to do this." Nobody ordered me not to. So I did it.

Here's what happened.

The TiVo integration worked. Customers loved it. We got the foothold we wanted.

Then the unexpected upside. Smart TV makers — Sony, Samsung, Vizio, Panasonic — saw the TiVo integration and wanted the same thing on their devices. Within weeks, we went from being a PC download service to being on every major living-room screen.

That was a step-change in the business. From a small engineering bet, we'd opened the entire living-room market.

When my director-promotion document went up, my boss's written evaluation said something like: "We should promote Ethan because without him, we wouldn't have this TiVo partnership and the doors it opened." That single line — that single bet — was the centerpiece of the case for promotion.

I made it from senior manager to director in two years at Amazon. That's fast for that level. The TiVo bet is what made it fast.

### What I want you to take from this

Three things:

1. **The bet was calculated, not reckless.** I had analyzed the engineering cost. I knew it was 7% of resources, not 70%. I knew the downside was modest. I wasn't gambling — I was taking a risk.

2. **The bet went against the room.** Most of the people in the meeting didn't want to do it. I had to be willing to disagree. Without that willingness, the bet doesn't happen and the promotion doesn't happen.

3. **The unexpected upside was where the real value came from.** I didn't predict the smart TV adoption. But because I made the initial bet, the door was open when the upside materialized. You can't capture upside through doors you don't walk through.

### My one regret

Despite this story, my honest self-assessment is that I was TOO risk-averse over my career. I waited too long on too many bets. I stayed at Amazon longer than I should have. The lesson I'd give my younger self: take more bets, sooner. Don't romanticize the few I took. Take the others I didn't.

The cost of risk-aversion is invisible. You don't see the doors that closed because you didn't walk through. You only see the comfortable continuity. But promotion is asymmetric — one big calculated win can outweigh five years of consistent reliability.

## Focus only on the risks you can control

Here's another reframe that's useful for people who get anxious about risk-taking.

There are risks you can't control: war, recession, pandemic, natural disaster, competitive moves, regulatory change. These will happen. They will hit your business. You don't control them.

There are risks you can control: how you respond, what backup plans you have, what redundancy you build, what relationships you've cultivated, how prepared you are.

**Focus only on the controllable risks.**

The uncontrollable risks are real, but they're the same for everyone in your industry. What separates leaders is how they prepare for and respond to those risks — which is itself a controllable thing.

Don't waste mental energy on whether the recession will hit. Spend it on: if a recession hits, what's my response plan? Who do I need to call? What costs do I cut first? What gets me to the other side?

That's leadership thinking.

## Reframe: solve the problem, not the request

Quick aside, but related to risk-taking. When someone asks you for something, don't just take the request literally. Ask: what's the underlying problem they're trying to solve?

Classic example: in 1900, if you asked customers what they wanted, they'd say "a faster horse." But the problem they were really solving was transportation. The car solved the problem; the faster horse didn't.

Same dynamic in 1960s appliances: customers asked for "hotter ovens." The problem was cooking time. The microwave solved the problem; a hotter oven didn't.

When you're working on a strategic bet, ask the deeper question. Don't just solve what you've been told to solve. Solve the underlying problem. That's where the calculated risk often is — and where the unexpected upside lives.

## Managing failure — the App Store crisis

You will fail at some bets. That's the nature of risk-taking. The question is how you handle the failure, because handling it well preserves the trust that lets you take more bets.

Let me tell you about a real failure I managed at Amazon.

We were launching the Amazon App Store. Big deal. Jeff Bezos had personally signed a customer-facing letter announcing the launch. The letter was scheduled to go live Friday morning at 6am Pacific Time. Everything had to work.

The launch was overnight, which was Mistake #1. Everyone was tired. When something went wrong, the team had less capacity to fix it.

The actual technical error was elementary. We had a database call inline with a button display. In testing — small load, fast database — it worked fine. In production — millions of users hitting it at once — the database calls backed up, buttons didn't render, the page broke.

Bezos discovers the letter isn't live. He emails. The chain escalates rapidly. All my management is copied. The clock is ticking. Customers can't access the App Store.

Here's how I handled it.

### Step 1: Own it

I wrote back to Bezos within minutes:

> "Jeff, you're right. It's a problem. We're working on it. I will get this fixed."

No excuses. No blame-shifting. No "the database vendor failed us" or "this was actually unprecedented load." Just: yes, this is broken, I own it, I'm fixing it.

This is the most important step. The instinct in a crisis is to explain why it happened and what could have been done differently. Suppress that instinct in the first communication. Just own it.

### Step 2: Communicate clearly and frequently

I followed up:

> "Here's what we're doing this hour. I will check back in at 7am with status."

Cadence and specificity. The senior leader needs to know: somebody's on it, the work is happening, and they don't have to ask for the next update because it's coming on a known schedule.

This is what differentiates good crisis management. Bad crisis management goes silent under pressure. Good crisis management increases communication frequency.

### Step 3: Fix the actual problem

Obvious. Get the immediate problem solved. Don't get into root-cause analysis until the customer-facing impact is over.

### Step 4: Send a real post-mortem after

Once the crisis is over, do the analysis. Document what happened, what the contributing factors were, what we'd change. Send it broadly. Don't bury it.

### Step 5: Don't repeat the same kind of failure

The unforgivable failure isn't the first one. It's the second one of the same kind. If I'd had another launch a few months later that broke for the same reason, that would have been career-damaging. The first one was managed. The second one wouldn't have been.

### What this whole arc taught me

Crisis recovery is one of the highest-leverage skills you can build. Most people perform okay in normal conditions and dramatically worse under pressure. If you can perform well — calmly, clearly, decisively — under pressure, you stand out.

This is also where senior leadership decides who they trust at the next level. Anyone can look fine when things are going well. The question is what you look like at 3am when everything's broken. That's the test.

## Tactically impatient, strategically patient

This is a Bezos-attributed line that's stuck with me.

- **Tactically impatient** — push hard now. Move fast on the things you can move fast on. Don't accept slowness when speed is available.
- **Strategically patient** — let the long arc unfold. Don't panic about timelines that span years.

Both at once. Push hard this week, this month, this quarter. But don't catastrophize about whether the promotion comes 6 or 12 months later than you wanted. The 6-month difference is not what determines your career.

I see senior managers ruin their careers two ways:

1. **Strategically impatient** — they panic about not being promoted. They make rash moves. They leave companies prematurely. They damage relationships chasing immediate fixes.
2. **Tactically patient** — they accept slowness in the day-to-day. They let weeks slip without making progress. They wait for things to come to them.

The right pattern is the inverse. Push hard daily. Be patient about the long timeline.

## Putting it together

1. Trust and risk are in tension. Resolve it by taking calculated risks, not gambles.
2. The 70% rule for most decisions. The 90% rule for one-way doors.
3. The four-step risk framework: alternatives, data, test, undo plan.
4. Take the bet. The TiVo story shows what one bet can do for promotion.
5. Failure happens. Own it fast, communicate frequently, fix it, post-mortem, don't repeat.
6. Tactically impatient, strategically patient.

The biggest mistake I see is people thinking trust and risk are opposites. They're not. Trust is what lets you take risks. Risks — done well — are what build the next level of trust. The cycle compounds in your favor if you do it right.

## Action items

- Identify the calculated risk you've been avoiding for the last six months. What's the smallest version of it you can run? Run that this quarter.
- Sort your three current biggest decisions: which are one-way doors, which are two-way? Make sure you're moving at the right speed for each.
- Run the four-step risk framework on your next significant decision. Write it down. Don't shortcut a step.
- For your most likely failure mode at work — the project that could go wrong — pre-write the crisis response. What would you say in the first email? Who would you copy? What's the cadence? Have it ready.
- Ask yourself: am I tactically patient where I should be impatient? Or strategically impatient where I should be patient? Which side do I tend toward?
