# Promotion and Scope: How It Actually Works

> Distilled from Ethan Evans's "Breakthrough at Senior Manager" course (Day 2). What's actually being judged in the promotion room, why scope is a gating criterion, and the six ways scope gets created.

If you want to get promoted to director or VP, you need to understand the actual mechanism. Most senior managers don't. They think it's a meritocracy where the best work wins. It is not. It is a process — a structured, somewhat-conservative, multi-stakeholder process — and you have to play it on its terms, not the terms you wish were in play.

Let me walk you through how it actually works.

---

## The three judgments every promotion answers

Every executive promotion comes down to three questions, asked in the room. Sometimes simultaneously, sometimes one at a time, but all three eventually.

### Judgment 1: Can you do the job?

Are you qualified? Do you demonstrate executive performance — the three personal standards (scaled+deep, change agent, trusted)? Do you have the executive presence? Do you make good decisions? Do you have the soft skills?

This is the half of the equation everyone focuses on. Make yourself promotable. That's most of what coaches and books talk about.

But it's only one of three.

### Judgment 2: Is the scope there?

Even if you're qualified, is the work you're doing big enough to justify a director's title and salary? This is where a lot of qualified people get stuck.

A director typically has more headcount, more P&L responsibility, more complexity, and broader reach than a senior manager. The numbers vary by company — at some places a director is 50 people, at others 200, at others it's about complexity not headcount. But there's a numeric or scope-based threshold somewhere.

If the work isn't big enough, you don't get promoted. Even if you'd be a great director. The company doesn't pay director-level salaries for senior-manager-sized jobs.

### Judgment 3: Is the executive need there?

This is the most subtle one and the one that surprises people.

Even if you're qualified and the scope is there, is the role actually hard enough to justify paying for executive leadership? Some roles are operationally complex but not strategically valuable. Running a warehouse — hundreds of people, lots of operational complexity, real responsibility — but at most companies that doesn't get a director title. The complexity is high; the strategic ambiguity is low.

The executive need formula is roughly: **ambiguity × complexity × impact.**

- Ambiguity: how uncertain is the space? How much judgment does it require?
- Complexity: how hard is the actual problem?
- Impact: will solving this move the needle on revenue, profit, customers, strategic position?

If all three are high, the role needs an executive. If one of them is low — even if the others are high — the company will often staff it with a senior manager and a process.

This is why promotions to senior IC and senior PM are usually easier than promotions to director. There's a known need for senior ICs and senior PMs. There's a much spottier, more contested need for new directors. Especially in low-growth environments.

### The "10x problem" metric

David Glick at Walmart, an SVP I respect, gives this advice: "Solve a 10x problem."

If the problem you're working on can 10x customers, revenue, transactions, or sales, you have an executive-need-sized problem. If your problem is 10% improvement on something — even if it's hard, even if you do it well — you're not at the next level. You're optimizing within the level.

Ask yourself: what's the 10x problem in my space? Am I working on it? If not, who is, and how do I get aligned with that work?

## The promotion panel — who's actually in the room

Here's the thing most people miss. Your manager doesn't promote you alone. There's a panel.

At Amazon, the process was called OLR — Organization and Leadership Review. Different companies have different names. Google has panel processes. Each company has some variation. But the structure is the same: your manager writes a document about you, attaches peer feedback, and presents to a room full of decision-makers.

That room typically includes:
- Your manager (or skip, depending on level being promoted to)
- Your manager's peers
- Your skip-level (your manager's manager)
- Sometimes your skip's peers, depending on how high the promotion goes

Your manager isn't deciding alone. They're advocating, and the room is voting or building consensus.

### The strategic implication

If you don't know who is going to be in that room, you are playing blind. You need to know:

1. Who's going to be in your document — which peers will provide feedback?
2. Who's going to be in the room — which skip-level peers and managers will be reviewing?

If you can list both groups, you at least know what the chessboard looks like. You can start cultivating relationships with the people whose voices will matter.

If you can't list those people, your first move is to ask your manager: "Help me understand who's involved in this process. Who reviews documents like mine? Who else needs to know me?"

This isn't ambitious overreach. This is wise. Your manager wants to be right — they're being judged in the room too — so they have the same incentive you do for you to know the chessboard.

### Why your manager wants to be right

This is the lever most people miss. If your manager puts you up for promotion and the room turns you down, that's bad for them. It signals their judgment is off. So they're highly motivated to make sure your document is strong and the room is prepared.

Use that. Don't think of yourself as begging for support. Think of yourself as a partner with your manager in a process where you both want the same outcome. Frame the conversation that way:

"You and I both want this to land. Let's plan it together. Who's in the room? What concerns might they have? Who do I need to be in front of before this happens?"

## Why time matters — the track-record requirement

A common frustration: "I think I'm ready. Why am I not being put up?"

Here's the unromantic answer. Promotion processes are conservative by design. They're trying to figure out: are you lucky or are you good?

Did that big project you shipped — was that really you, or was that the team's inertia? The favorable conditions? The senior IC who carried it? Can you do it again?

The mechanism is designed for low error rates. One of the best ways to have a low error rate is more data. So they wait. They watch you do it again. And again. They want a track record.

At Amazon, the average dwell time at senior manager was 60 months. Five years. That's the average. Some faster, some slower.

For VP, I don't have hard stats but I have observations. Fastest I ever saw director-to-VP was 18 months — and that was because the person had been held back at senior manager too long, and the company corrected by skipping director-equivalent dwell. Slowest was 9 years. I personally took six.

So when your manager says "let's wait six months, let's wait until you've delivered another project" — they're trying to accumulate evidence. They want to be able to say in the room: "Ethan has now delivered four projects over six years at this level of complexity. He can do it repeatably. He can do it reliably. Let's promote."

You'll be the first person to think you're ready. Almost everyone is. You're your own most forgiving judge. Your boss isn't paying as much attention to your career as you are. They notice maybe 50–80% of your good work. They notice 100% of your mistakes — or at least, they notice them clearly.

That's the asymmetry. Account for it.

## Scope: definition, mechanics, getting it

### What counts as scope

Scope is multi-dimensional. Don't reduce it to headcount, but headcount is the most visible. The full picture:

- **Headcount.** Most companies use this as a primary signal. Roughly 25–35 reports for senior manager, 100+ for director (varies a lot by function and company). Visible. Easy to count. Companies optimize for what's easy to count.
- **P&L ownership.** Are you directly accountable for revenue or profit? This is a strong strategic signal — if you own the P&L, you're treated as a real executive.
- **Complexity.** For ICs and especially senior ICs, scope is mostly problem difficulty, not headcount. Are you solving problems no one else at your level can solve?
- **Scale of service.** Are you serving a billion users? Running a trillion-dollar business flow? Operating at scale changes everything about how leadership is required.
- **Cross-functional reach, international scope, autonomy.** All count.

### Why headcount isn't the whole story

There are real reasons headcount matters: more people = more leadership challenges, communication scales nonlinearly, complexity emerges at scale.

There are also bad reasons headcount matters: empire-building incentives, easy to measure, leaders compare org charts. This is broken, but it's reality.

So don't ignore headcount. But don't think headcount alone makes the case. You need the other dimensions too.

## The six ways scope gets created

Now to the practical question: how do you actually get scope?

There are six paths. They are roughly ranked by desirability and ease, but the right path for you depends on your situation.

### Path 1: Organic growth (the ideal)

The cleanest path: your job grows to next-level complexity because the business grows.

When I joined Amazon, my first team was six people. By the time I made VP, my team was 800. Amazon grew 100-fold in headcount during my time there. So my team was always growing organically. I didn't need to wait for a manager to leave or to take on someone else's portfolio. The work itself was getting bigger.

If your company is in this kind of growth phase, you're lucky. Stay where you are, work hard, and the scope will come.

If your company isn't growing — and most of the big tech companies right now aren't — this path is closed for you. Then you need a different path.

### Path 2: Manager leaves (the Klingon promotion)

Sometimes a hole opens. Your manager gets promoted, leaves the company, gets reassigned, or — yes — performance-managed out. Their job needs filling. You're the obvious internal candidate.

I call this the Klingon promotion because Klingons are promoted when their commanding officer is killed. Same idea, less violent.

This is real. It happens. But you can't plan it, and you can't count on it. And when it happens, you're competing with all the other senior managers who reported to the same person. Whoever has the strongest manager-relationship-in-the-org-above wins.

If you have a great relationship with your skip-level, this path is more available. If you don't, it isn't.

My first promotion at Amazon was essentially a Klingon promotion. My boss wanted to leave, the company didn't want to lose both of us, I made it clear I'd leave too if I wasn't moving up — and they made the call.

### Path 3: Reorg or layoff (divisive but real)

In downturns, layoffs happen. When someone is laid off, their team gets distributed. Their workstreams need new owners. Leaders parcel out the pieces as "prizes" — to retain top performers, to grow people, to test capability.

If you have trust with your manager and skip-level when this happens, you'll get pieces. If you don't, someone else will.

This path opened up a lot of director roles in 2023–2024. It's an unhappy path because it requires someone else to lose their job, but it's a real one.

### Path 4: Take on unstaffed work (broaden your remit)

This is where you start creating scope yourself. There are things in the org that aren't owned. Or are owned by someone too overloaded to do them well. Or are technically owned but no one's actually moving them forward.

You go to your manager and say: "I see X is sitting on the table. No one's driving it. I'd like to pick it up." Or you just pick it up — you start doing the work — and three months later your team is doing more than the org chart suggests.

The trade-off: you work longer hours and have a broader remit before you have headcount or title to match. You're paying upfront for scope you'll get later.

### Path 5: Start a new initiative

Invent the work. Propose a new project, get it funded, build the team to deliver it. This is the highest-risk path because if the initiative fails, you've spent capital without returns. But if it works, you've created scope from nothing.

I created a t-shirt printing business inside Amazon. My job was to run the app store. The t-shirt thing was not in my job description. I had to fight for the funding, prove the model, build the team. It became Merch on Demand, a real business at Amazon. It grew my scope.

I was also part of starting Prime Gaming — the gaming tier of Amazon Prime, comparable to Prime Video and Prime Music. Same playbook. Propose new business, champion it, build it.

This requires a tolerance for risk and an ability to sell internally. It's not for everyone. But the people who do this and succeed get promoted faster than the people who don't.

### Path 6: Dirty work — take on what no one else wants

This is the least glamorous path and arguably the most reliable.

I had a non-technical boss for a stretch at Amazon who would periodically ask me: "Can you take on this team?" The teams were unrelated and not always exciting. Video game delivery. Software sales. Trade-in logistics. B2B. Wireless. Reverse logistics. None of it was glamorous, none of it was a career-defining-mission area.

I kept saying yes.

After two or three of these absorptions, I had over 200 people reporting to me. The portfolio was a hodgepodge — none of it was the prestige work — but the headcount was undeniably director-sized. The conversation about my promotion to VP started naturally because the math required it.

The dirty-work strategy works because most people don't take it. They want the prestige assignments. So the dirty work sits there. If you take it, you get scope without competing with as many people for it.

It's effective. It's not exciting. Decide for yourself if you can stomach it.

## How to know what your company specifically requires

The general framework is universal. The specifics vary.

Step one: ask your manager directly. "What did the last director-level promotion look like in our org? What was their scope, their named impact, their relationships?"

Step two: ask your skip-level. "I'm working toward director. From your view, what does someone at that level need to look like in this org? What's the readiness signal you'd want to see?"

Step three: observe. Who's getting promoted? Why are they being valued? What did they do that you can copy or differentiate against?

If you can't answer "what does this company specifically expect of a director in this function," you're guessing about the target. Stop guessing. Find out.

## A note on hard times

If you're at a company that isn't growing, the scope path is harder. There's no organic growth. Manager-leaves is rare. Reorg is the main mechanism, and it's involuntary. New initiatives are hard to fund. Dirty work still works but the dirty work pile is smaller.

In low-growth environments, your two main moves are:

1. Build skills and trust now so you're the obvious choice when growth resumes. Use the slow time as muscle-building time.
2. Consider that high-growth roles may be at other companies. We talk about that in the career strategy file.

But don't panic. The big tech companies have alternated between growth and stagnation many times. I joined Amazon in 2005 thinking I was joining a slow-growth large company; over the next 15 years it grew 100x. The cycle turns.

A career is 30 years. A 12-month slowdown is 3% of that.

## Putting it together

1. Promotion is judged on three dimensions: can you do the job, is the scope there, is the executive need real.
2. The decision is made by a panel, not by your manager alone. Know who's in the room.
3. Time matters because the system is conservative — it wants evidence, not just performance.
4. Scope can come six ways: organic growth, manager leaves, reorg, unstaffed work, new initiative, dirty work.
5. The right path for you depends on your company, your environment, and your appetite.

The biggest mistake I see is people working hard at their current scope and waiting to be noticed. That's a strategy that worked when companies were growing fast. In a slower environment, it's not enough. You have to actively shape your scope.

Pick a path. Move on it.

---

## Action items

- Make a list of who's likely to be in your promotion room. If you can't, ask your manager.
- Identify your "10x problem." If you don't have one, that's the gap.
- Audit yourself against the three judgments: qualified? scope? executive need? Which one is weakest?
- Pick the most plausible scope path for your situation. Move on it this quarter.
- Ask your manager: "What did the last successful director-level promotion in our org look like? What did they have that I should be building toward?"
