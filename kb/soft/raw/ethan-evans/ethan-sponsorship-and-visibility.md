# Sponsorship, Networking, and Visibility — Building Power Before You Need It

> Distilled from Ethan Evans's "Breakthrough at Senior Manager" course (Day 2 + PreWork). The difference between sponsors and mentors, the SVP mentor strategy that got me through promotion, why volume trumps quality on visibility, and how to build a network without it being transactional.

You can be qualified, you can have the scope, you can have your manager on side — and you can still not get promoted. Why? Because the room makes the decision, and you don't have anyone in the room other than your manager who can speak to your work.

Sponsorship and visibility are about building the relationships that put advocates in those rooms. This is one of the most under-invested areas for technical leaders, especially introverts. It's also one of the highest-leverage.

---

## Sponsor versus mentor — get the distinction right

Most people use these words interchangeably. They are not the same thing.

**A mentor educates and advises you.** They share wisdom. They tell you what they've learned. They give you a sounding board. The mentor's value is the conversation itself.

**A sponsor advocates for you in rooms you're not in.** They expend their own credibility on your behalf. They make introductions. They argue for you when you're not there. The sponsor's value is the action they take when you're absent.

You need both. They serve different purposes. A mentor helps you get smarter. A sponsor helps you get promoted.

The mistake I see is people heavily over-investing in mentors and under-investing in sponsors. Mentors are easier to acquire — you ask, they say yes, you have coffee. Sponsors require more — they have to actually believe in you enough to spend their credibility on you. And they have to be the right level to be in the rooms that matter.

## The SVP mentor story — how I got through promotion

About a year before I made VP at Amazon, my own VP gave me what turned out to be the most valuable career advice I got there. He said:

> "You should try to get an SVP as a mentor. It would look really good. The SVPs are the people in the room when your VP promotion document is reviewed. If one of them has been your mentor, there will be someone who knows you when your name comes up."

He helped me set it up. I got Brian Valentine — at the time a senior leader who had come from Microsoft, gone to Amazon, and later did other things. The technical details don't matter. The point is: an SVP, two levels above me, agreed to mentor me.

We had a series of meetings. Substantive ones. Real career discussions, real strategic thinking. Brian got to know me well enough to have an opinion of my work.

When my VP promotion document went up — to the room with Jeff Bezos and his S-team — there was a person in the room who said: "Oh yes, I mentor Ethan. Here's what I think of him." That was my advocate.

This is what I mean by sponsor as distinct from mentor. The mentor work was the conversations. The sponsor work was speaking up in the room I was nowhere near. Brian did both.

That single relationship probably moved my promotion by months, possibly more.

### The implication

If you're aiming for a promotion at level N, you want a relationship — a real, substantive one — with someone at level N+1 or N+2 in your org. Someone who will be in or adjacent to the room when your name is discussed.

This takes time to build. You can't show up two weeks before promotion season and ask for help. You build the relationship over a year or more.

## How to actually engage executive sponsors

Most people approach senior leaders the wrong way. They show up to "build a relationship" and ask vague questions about career advice. The senior leader spends 30 minutes being polite, the conversation produces nothing, and the relationship doesn't deepen.

Here's how to do it right.

### Bring value first

This is the rule. Bring value before you ask for anything.

What does that look like? You bring information they don't have. You teach them something. You give them perspective from a level they can't see directly. You introduce them to someone they'd find valuable. You make their job easier in some small way.

Then — and only then — you ask.

### The 30/10 rule

For mentor meetings, my rule is: in a 30-minute meeting, 10 minutes should be me teaching them something they don't know. The rest can be mentorship.

This sounds counterintuitive. They're senior to me. What do I know that they don't? Plenty, actually. They don't know what's happening at my level day-to-day. They don't know the specific dynamics in my team. They don't see the on-the-ground perspective on initiatives they care about. I bring that to the conversation.

This makes me valuable to them. It also makes the meeting a real exchange instead of a one-way information flow. Senior leaders enjoy this. They get tired of people pretending to want their wisdom when they really just want their influence.

### Make it specific to their expertise

When you bring value, tailor it. Don't show up with generic information. Show up with something that hits their specific area.

If they care about platform performance, bring an insight about platform performance. If they care about a particular product line, bring a perspective on that product line. If they care about hiring, bring something about hiring.

This requires research. Know what they care about before you walk in.

### Build incrementally

You don't make the big ask in the first meeting. You build over multiple meetings. By the third or fourth conversation, you have credibility. Now you can make a more substantive ask.

Pace yourself.

### Then make the specific ask

Once the relationship is real, make the ask. The language can be:

> "I really value our conversations. As you know, I'm working toward [next level]. Will you help me? Will you speak up for me when my name comes up in promotion discussions? Will you make introductions to people I should know?"

That's a real ask. It's clear. It's specific. It's something they can say yes or no to.

Most people never make this ask. They hope the senior leader will figure out on their own that they want help. They won't. You have to ask.

## Don't be coin-operated

There's a failure mode I want to flag. Some people approach networking as a pure transaction. They take what they need from the relationship and disappear. Then years later, they show up needing something else, and they're surprised when the relationship isn't warm.

I had a senior manager ask me to mentor him. I agreed. He came once. Asked a lot of questions. Took the answers. Disappeared — no follow-up, no thanks, nothing.

Five years later, that same manager wanted to join my team. I had standing to bring him over. I did. I worked with him. I helped get him promoted to a level he wanted. But the dynamic is exactly the same as it was years ago — he responds to my emails when I have something to offer him. The moment I'm less relevant to his career, the responses stop.

That's coin-operated. That's the kind of person you don't want to be.

The relationships that actually matter — the ones that get you promoted, get you opportunities you wouldn't otherwise see, get you saved when something goes wrong — those are the relationships where you've put in real investment over time. You've helped people without expectation. You've stayed in touch when you didn't need anything. You've been there for them.

Those relationships have durability. The transactional ones don't.

This isn't a moral judgment, although you can read it that way. It's a pragmatic point. Coin-operated relationships fail you exactly when you need them most.

## Networking — building power before you need it

The wisdom from a friend of mine: "When's the best time to plant a tree? Twenty years ago. When's the second-best time? Today."

Same with networking. The best time to build your network was several years ago. The second-best time is now. Either way, start.

### What networking actually is

Most people think networking means cocktail parties and LinkedIn requests. That's a bad model. Networking is just connecting authentically with people, sharing value, and building real bonds over time.

It's not about the volume of contacts. It's about the depth and breadth of relationships where there's actual mutual investment.

### The four steps for building a network

1. **Bring value first.** Same rule as sponsorship. Teach people something. Share what you know. Introduce them to someone they'd find useful. Don't approach with an ask — approach with an offer.

2. **Then ask.** Once you've given, people are far more likely to help you. They want to reciprocate. They feel known to you, not just used.

3. **Make it easy for them.** People genuinely like helping. They like being seen as helpful. The easier you make it for them to help you — the more specific your ask, the more time-boxed, the more clear — the more likely they'll do it.

4. **Network within your company before outside.** People at your own company already share commonality with you. The friction is lower. Start there. Outside-company networking is more important later, but inside is where to start.

### The Expedia story — why networking actually works

Here's the story I tell to make this concrete.

Years ago, I traveled to Ukraine to deliver medical aid. On the way back, my flight got cancelled. I was stuck. Customer service at Expedia (where I had booked) was useless. Hours on the phone, no progress.

I posted the situation to my network. Just shared the story. Within six hours, Expedia was calling ME. Offering free miles, upgrades, paths home. I didn't know anyone at Expedia personally. But somebody in my network knew somebody at Expedia and tagged them. Then someone else tagged someone else. The ask spread, multiple people worked on the problem, and Expedia found me.

That's networking. Not me at a cocktail party. Not me on LinkedIn. Just real relationships with real people, with enough breadth that someone-knew-someone for almost any problem I might have.

The key insight: **for what's impossible for you, there's someone for whom it's easy.** A friend, a connection, a friend of a friend. Networking lets you find those people quickly when you need them.

### How long does it take

It doesn't take that long. I built my network in about three years of deliberate effort. I wish I'd known to start twenty years earlier, but three years was enough to make a real difference.

### For introverts

You don't have to work cocktail parties. You can build a network through:
- LinkedIn — direct, asynchronous, low-pressure
- 1:1 coffees — focused, no awkward group dynamics
- Substantive 1:1s with people you respect — not transactional
- Carpool, elevator, alumni networks, shared interests — opportunistic but real
- Writing — share what you know publicly, people who care will find you

The mode doesn't matter. The consistency does.

If asking people for help is hard for you, read *Rejection Proof* by Jia Jiang. Or watch his YouTube series. He's good. He spent a hundred days deliberately seeking rejection so he'd lose the fear of asking. It's funny, useful, and it works.

## Visibility — getting noticed without arrogance

Sponsorship and networking are the relationship side of being known. Visibility is the broadcast side. They work together.

The fundamental problem: most senior managers are working hard quietly and hoping to be noticed. That's not a strategy. It's a wish. Hard work alone doesn't equal visibility, especially in big orgs where there are lots of people doing hard work.

You have to do BOTH: deliver well AND make the work visible.

### Volume trumps quality

Here's the principle that surprises people: when it comes to staying visible, volume matters more than quality.

Why? Because promotion decisions are heavily affected by recency bias. People remember what they saw recently. If you do one big thing in March and then go quiet for the rest of the year, by November the promotion conversation is dominated by what people remember from October. Not March.

If you do small things — small wins, small updates, small visibility moments — every week or every two weeks, you stay top-of-mind. Even if individually they're not as impressive as your one big thing.

This is uncomfortable for people who feel that broadcasting small wins is somehow self-promoting or unseemly. Get over it. The alternative — doing great work that no one remembers — is worse.

### The work-in-silence trap

If you're working hard and hoping to be noticed, you're not doing your full job. The full job at this level includes making sure your work is visible. That's not vanity; that's a real responsibility.

Think about it from your manager's side. They have many reports, all working hard. If one person is serving their list of accomplishments to your manager on a silver platter every week, and the other is laboring quietly in obscurity, even a fair manager will end up better-informed about the first person. They'll talk about the first person's work in promotion discussions because that's the work they remember.

This isn't because the manager is bad. It's because human attention is finite.

### Practical visibility tactics

Here are some moves that work:

- **Weekly or biweekly written updates to your manager.** Not just status — accomplishments. Wins. Things you're proud of. They forward these. They quote them. They use them in calibration.
- **Speak up in meetings, even when not asked.** Don't dominate. But don't be silent. Pick one or two moments per meeting where you contribute something substantive.
- **Publish team accomplishments broadly.** Email lists, Slack channels, internal newsletters. Make sure your team's wins are visible org-wide, not just to your direct chain.
- **Cross-team work.** Be visibly involved in initiatives that span multiple teams. People you don't normally work with see you in action.
- **Conferences and external presentations.** If your company values external visibility, do it. Even one talk per year shifts your profile.

### Self-promotion is not arrogance

A lot of people I coach have a moral discomfort with promoting themselves. They feel like it's bragging. Like the work should speak for itself.

The work doesn't speak for itself. Especially at scale. There's too much going on for the work to be heard. Someone has to amplify it.

If you don't amplify your own work, no one else will. And if you do amplify it — well or with technique — it will be heard.

Self-promotion done badly is annoying. Self-promotion done well is just communication. The difference is in the technique:
- Lead with the impact, not your own role.
- Credit your team, not just yourself.
- Tie your work to outcomes the listener cares about.
- Don't repeat the same content to the same audience too often.
- Make it about the work, not about you.

That's not arrogance. That's leadership communication.

## Be bright, be brief, be gone

When you're interacting with executives or skip-levels, there's a great rule that came up in my career: **be bright, be brief, be gone.**

- **Bright** — bring something of value. Don't show up empty.
- **Brief** — don't take more time than necessary. They're busy.
- **Gone** — leave when the value is delivered. Don't linger trying to maximize face time.

The places senior people get frustrated:

- Reports who try to maximize time with them — "can we have a meeting? I'd love to talk." That's not value. That's an extraction.
- Reports who can't get to the point — five minutes of context for a one-minute ask.
- Reports who hang on after the substance is done.

Senior leaders are working at a pace and intensity you may not appreciate. When I was VP, an extra 30 minutes you took from me was 30 minutes I took from my family. So if you wanted my time, the math had to work — you brought enough value to justify the cost.

Frame your interactions accordingly. Have something concrete to discuss. Get to it quickly. Wrap up when it's wrapped. They will remember you fondly because you respected their time. They will not remember you fondly if you took too much of it.

## Skip-level relationships specifically

Your skip-level (your manager's manager) is a critical relationship. They're the person who likely makes or heavily influences your promotion. You need them to know you.

Tactics:
- **Use project moments to engage.** Don't request a 1:1 to "build a relationship." That's transparent. Instead, after a major project, ask for a debrief. "We just shipped X. I'd love to walk you through what worked and what didn't, and get your perspective on what we should do next." Specific, value-bringing, time-bounded.
- **Ask in context, not directly.** Don't ask "how can I get promoted." Ask "given X work my team is doing, what would you want to see more of? What patterns do you value at the next level?"
- **Find natural overlap moments.** Off-sites, all-hands, social events, hiring loops. Be deliberate about being present in those moments.
- **Don't substitute the skip relationship for the manager relationship.** Your manager is still primary. The skip-level is additive. If you start going around your manager, you damage the most important relationship you have.

## Putting it together

1. Mentors educate; sponsors advocate. You need both. Most people under-invest in sponsors.
2. Get a person in the room. Either your skip-level peers or someone two levels up via mentorship.
3. Bring value first. The 30/10 rule on mentor meetings.
4. Don't be coin-operated. Real relationships have durability that transactional ones don't.
5. Networking: plant the tree. Today, if not twenty years ago.
6. Volume trumps quality on visibility. Small frequent updates beat one big annual splash.
7. Be bright, be brief, be gone with executives.
8. Self-promotion done well is not arrogance. It's communication.

The thing about networking and visibility is they compound. Every relationship you build, every piece of visibility you create, makes the next one easier. The flip side is also true: the longer you wait, the more compound interest you've missed.

Start today. Plant the tree.

## Action items

- Identify two people two levels above you in your org. Pick one to start cultivating as a mentor-into-sponsor. Email them this week.
- Apply the 30/10 rule in your next senior-leader meeting. Prepare 10 minutes of value to bring before you ask anything.
- Write down five people in your network who'd be valuable to reconnect with. Send each one a value-add message this month — no ask, just contact.
- Stand up a regular visibility cadence — weekly or biweekly written update to your manager, monthly or quarterly broader update to your skip and beyond.
- Audit your last three executive interactions: were you bright, brief, and gone? Or did you linger?
