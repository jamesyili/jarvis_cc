# Feedback and Self-Assessment — Knowing Where You Actually Stand

> Distilled from Ethan Evans's "Breakthrough at Senior Manager" course (PreWork + Day 1). The four methods for knowing your real position, why we're our own most forgiving judges, and the perception-is-reality principle that closes the loop on most "unfair" feedback.

If you don't know where you stand, you're guessing about what to fix. So the first piece of work — before you can build a plan — is to honestly map where you actually are.

This is harder than it sounds. We are systematically bad at self-assessment. So this file is about the methods for getting around that.

---

## The fundamental asymmetry: intentions versus actions

If you take one phrase from this entire course, take this one:

**We judge ourselves by our intentions, but others by their actions.**

When you fail to ship on time, you know all the reasons. The engineer was out. The dependency slipped. The PM's spec was late. The reorg disrupted the team. You meant to ship on time. Your intentions were good. So you ask to be judged on those intentions — give me forgiveness for the things outside my control.

When someone else fails to ship on time, what do you do? You judge their results. You don't think about their intentions. You think "they didn't deliver." Maybe you give them a pass once. Maybe twice. But the running tally in your head is on the actions, not the intentions.

Now turn that around. When others assess you for promotion, they're judging your actions. Your shipped products. Your hires. Your retentions. Your visible behavior in meetings. They're not factoring in your intentions because they don't know them and they don't have time to work it out.

So when you feel the frustration of "I'm not being moved up and I don't understand why" — part of that frustration is the genuine asymmetry. You know you're working hard. You know your intentions are great. But that's not what they're seeing. They're seeing actions and outputs.

Account for this in two ways:

1. **Be your own harsher judge.** Stop giving yourself the intention-credit. Look at your actions the way an outsider would look at them.
2. **Make your actions visible.** If your good work isn't being seen, fix the visibility, not just the work.

## The four methods to know your position

There are four reliable ways to figure out where you actually stand. Use all four. Triangulate.

### Method 1: Ask your manager (most important)

Your manager is the single most influential person in your career. Their assessment of you carries the most weight. So you have to know what their assessment actually is.

The problem: most managers default to vague feedback. "You're doing great, keep it up." That's not actionable. That tells you nothing about what to change.

Here's a four-step technique to get past the vague answer.

**Step 1: Give advance notice.**

Don't ambush them. Two weeks before your next 1:1, send something like: "In our next 1:1 I'd like to spend 20 minutes on real feedback for me. Could you observe and take notes between now and then so we can have a substantive conversation?"

This does two things. It gives them time to actually pay attention to your work with feedback in mind. And it signals you're serious — this isn't going to be a casual exchange.

**Step 2: Make them comfortable.**

Most managers are bad at giving feedback because they're afraid the person will react badly. Manage their comfort proactively. Open the conversation with something like:

> "I really want to grow. If you give me actionable feedback with specific examples, I will take it well, even if I don't fully agree. I might want to discuss it, but I'm not going to get defensive."

Then do that. Don't get defensive. Even when it stings.

**Step 3: Push for specifics.**

If they default to vague — "you're doing great" — push gently:

> "I'm glad you're pleased with my work. But to grow, I really need specifics. Can you give me a specific situation where I could have done better? Or a specific area where you'd want me to invest more?"

Don't accept the vague answer. Push once. Push again if needed.

**Step 4: If they still won't go specific, move to the other methods.**

Some managers will not give you specific feedback. They might be conflict-averse. They might be too busy. They might not actually have specific feedback. If after pushing twice they still don't go specific, that's data. Move to peer and skip-level methods.

### Method 2: Ask peers and skip-level

Your manager is one data point. Get more.

Approach peers — both inside your team and outside it — with this language:

> "I'm working on my development. Can you give me your honest read on what I do well and where I have gaps? I'd appreciate the specifics. I'll take it well."

For skip-level peers, you can frame it more contextually:

> "I'm trying to be sure I understand how I'm doing across the org, not just from my immediate team. What's your read on my work? What would you want me to invest more in?"

This is awkward. Do it anyway. You'll be surprised how often people will give you real feedback if you ask.

**The 75% overlap rule.** I've done a lot of 360-style assessments where I interview eight to ten people about a leader. Here's what shows up: it's not 80 different pieces of feedback. The overlap is about 75%. Out of 30 strengths, four major themes emerge. Out of 30 areas-to-improve, again four major themes.

That overlap is the truth about you. It's the consensus view. You can argue with one person's perception. You can't argue with eight people saying the same thing.

If three or more peers tell you the same thing about a gap, it is real. Stop arguing about whether it's fair.

### Method 3: Observe promotion patterns

Look at the people who are getting promoted around you. What is leadership valuing in them?

This sounds passive. It isn't. It's one of the highest-leverage things you can do.

I'll tell you a story to illustrate.

Early in my career, before Amazon, I held VP-level roles at startups. They were small companies; the titles were inflated. But I had peers I thought were incompetent.

In one company, my peer was a product manager who was constantly negative — "we won't hit our dates," "we're being too optimistic," "this won't work." I thought he was disloyal. We were a startup; we had to be all-in. Why was the CEO so high on him?

Turns out the PM was right. He had more industry experience. He could see we were biting off more than we could chew. The CEO valued his judgment because his judgment was actually better than mine.

In another company, my peer was a salesperson who was selling things our software didn't quite do yet. I was furious. He was overcommitting us. The CEO loved him.

Turns out: we were a startup running out of money. The CEO valued the revenue stream. Without signed contracts, we couldn't raise the next round. The salesperson was solving the existential problem; engineering excellence wasn't the existential problem.

In both cases, my CEO valued someone I thought was bringing less value than me. And in both cases, I was wrong about who was bringing more value — because I was measuring on my axis (technical excellence, loyalty to the team) instead of theirs (industry insight, revenue).

**The lesson:** When you see someone getting promoted that you don't think deserves it, instead of being annoyed, ask: what does leadership see in this person that I don't see? What is being valued there?

The answer to that question tells you what's actually valued at the next level. Not what you wish was valued. What is actually valued.

### Method 4: Follow the investment (indirect signals)

Sometimes the verbal feedback is incomplete or unreliable. Follow the money instead. Or the resources. Or the attention.

Watch:
- Who's getting the cool projects?
- Who's getting headcount?
- Who's getting conference budget?
- Who's getting time with senior leaders?
- Who's getting brought into important meetings?

If those things aren't going to you, who are they going to and why? The deduction is harder, but it's more reliable than what people say.

This also reveals your manager's biases — sometimes more than they'd articulate verbally. If your manager keeps giving the strategic projects to one peer and the operational projects to another, that tells you something about how they read each of you.

## Why people leave teams: the 70% rule

This isn't strictly about your self-assessment, but it is one of the most important pieces of feedback most managers ignore.

Research data shows that 70% of people who leave a team leave because of conflict with their direct manager.

Now, they won't tell you that when they're leaving. They'll say "I have a better offer" or "I want to try something new." But the researchers who came up with this number went back to people two years after they left. By then, with distance, people were honest.

70% left because of issues with their manager. Two-thirds.

If you're a manager, that means: when people are leaving your team, two out of three of them are leaving because of you.

I'm not pointing fingers. Two out of three of the people who left my own team over my career left because of something I did or didn't do. Maybe I wasn't supporting their career fast enough. Maybe I wasn't giving them the right work. Maybe I wasn't communicating well enough. Whatever it was — own it.

If you have attrition, look in the mirror first. The investigation almost always points back partly at you.

## Perception is reality, even when feedback feels wrong

This is the hardest reframe in the feedback file. You will hear feedback that feels wrong to you. Maybe completely wrong. Your instinct is to reject it.

Here's the rule:

**Every time you receive feedback, either (A) the feedback is accurate and you have a real gap, or (B) the person giving it has a perception that you have a gap. If they're in any position of power or influence, perception (B) is also a problem you have to address.**

You don't get to dismiss feedback as "they don't understand me." Even if they don't understand you, that's still a problem. Why don't they understand you? What's giving them that wrong impression? What can you change about your behavior or communication so they see you accurately?

I'll give you a personal example. I once received feedback that I was "disorganized." This made me angry. I had — and have — one of the tightest-run operations I knew of. Every meeting had an agenda. Every project had a tracker. Every quarter had clear goals.

But the person who said this had a perception. And they were senior to me. So I had to address it.

What I figured out: my emails were dense. My slides had too much on them. My meetings, while well-run, sometimes covered too much ground. The PERCEPTION of disorganization came from my outputs being information-dense, not from actual disorganization.

So I changed my outputs. Cleaner emails. Simpler slides. Tighter meeting agendas. The "disorganized" perception faded over the next year. The underlying organization didn't change. The presentation of it did.

The point: even when feedback feels factually wrong, there's almost always something to address. Find what.

### How to receive feedback that feels wrong

When someone gives you feedback that feels off, do not argue in the moment. Even if they're being unfair. Even if they're factually wrong.

The technique:

1. **Thank them** for the feedback, even if it stings.
2. **Ask for examples.** "Can you walk me through a specific situation where you saw this?" This converts vague to concrete. It also tests whether they actually have evidence or are running on impression.
3. **Paraphrase back.** "What I'm hearing is X. Is that the right read?" People love feeling understood. This builds the relationship even if you don't agree on the substance.
4. **Take time before responding substantively.** Sleep on it. Often the feedback you reject in the moment looks different 24 hours later.
5. **Close the loop later.** Even if you don't fully agree, find one thing you can adjust. Make that adjustment visible. Tell the person: "I thought about what you said. Here's what I'm changing."

That last step — closing the loop — is what builds trust over time. People remember that you took the feedback seriously, even if you didn't agree.

### A specific example of closing the loop publicly

I once posted on LinkedIn about five creators worth following. All five were men. Someone commented: "Are there no women worth following?"

My instinct could have been to defend the post. "These were the people most relevant to my immediate audience." Etc.

Instead, I acknowledged the gap publicly. I committed to compile a list of female leaders I follow. I followed up later with the list.

The reaction wasn't "Ethan was wrong, he's been called out." It was "Seems like a good guy. Acknowledged the issue and addressed it."

Closing the loop publicly turned a critique into a relationship-builder. That's the move.

## The "where am I stuck" diagnostic

Beyond the four methods above, here's a more focused diagnostic for senior managers trying to figure out why they're stuck.

Rank your top three reasons. Be honest. Some candidates:

- **Relationships.** You're brilliant and delivering, but you're hard to relate to. People don't enjoy working with you. At higher levels, this is fatal.
- **Unresponsive manager.** You have a manager who doesn't fight for you, doesn't carry your story, doesn't help you build skip-level relationships.
- **Insufficient scope.** You're at a small team or a stagnant area. There's no path up because there's nothing to grow into.
- **Executive presence gap.** You've been told (or you suspect) that you don't read as a leader at the next level. Vague feedback, hard to fix.
- **Unclear on what's needed.** You don't actually know what the next-level rubric is. You're flying blind.
- **You're not thinking big.** You execute well at your current level but you're not articulating a vision or pushing for change beyond what you're asked.
- **You're not innovating.** You're improving the existing thing rather than inventing the next thing.

Rank yours. The top one is your work for the next 6–12 months.

If you can't rank them — if you don't actually know which is the gap — go run the four feedback methods until you do. Don't operate on guesses.

## Self-assessment — set aside your ego

Here's the hardest part. We all want to be great. We got top marks in school. We probably went to good colleges and grad schools. Our self-image is "I'm a strong performer." That's why we made it this far.

The biggest blocker most senior managers have is they're not actually listening to the feedback. They're filtering it through "I'm a strong performer, so this feedback must be exaggerated or wrong."

Set the ego aside. Just for the diagnostic.

Assume — at least temporarily — that the feedback is accurate. Run the implications. What would be true if the feedback were correct? What would you change?

You can always go back and discount the feedback later. But run the experiment first. Most of the time, the feedback has at least a kernel of truth in it. Find that kernel.

The people I've coached who got unstuck the fastest are the ones who could honestly say: "Maybe I'm not as good as I think I am at X. Let me work on it." The ones who stay stuck are the ones who insist they're fine and the world is misperceiving them.

## Putting it together

1. We judge ourselves by intentions and others by actions. Account for this asymmetry.
2. Four methods for knowing where you stand: ask your manager (with the four-step technique), ask peers and skip-level, observe who's being promoted, follow the investment.
3. The 75% overlap rule: when multiple people say the same thing, it's true.
4. Perception is reality. Even feedback that feels wrong contains something to address.
5. When feedback comes in: thank, ask for examples, paraphrase, take time, close the loop.
6. Set the ego aside long enough to run the diagnostic. You can always re-evaluate later.

The thing about self-assessment is it's free and it's available. Most senior managers don't do it because it's uncomfortable. The ones who do — and who act on what they find — get unstuck.

## Action items

- Run the four-step feedback technique with your manager in your next 1:1. See what changes.
- Pick three peers and one skip-level peer. Ask them in the next two weeks: "What's your honest read on me — strengths, gaps, advice?"
- Look at the last three promotions in your org. What did each person have that you don't? Write it down.
- Make a list of every piece of feedback you've gotten in the last year that felt wrong to you. Run the "even if it's wrong, what's the perception causing it?" question on each.
- Rank your top three blockers honestly. Decide which is the one to work on first.
