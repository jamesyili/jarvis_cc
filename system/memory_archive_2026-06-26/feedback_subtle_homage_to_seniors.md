When James asks for "homage," "credit," or a nod to senior stakeholders in a message or artifact, default to **subtle lineage attribution** — not performative thanks.

**Why:** Session 2026-04-18c, drafting the Feedback Curator + Skeptic pre-share message to Andrew + Dylan. James asked for credit to Andrew's `quality_patterns.md` insight and Dylan's thread, explicitly noting: *"I'm brown-nosing; just add that small touch."* The tell — he flagged the anti-pattern (brown-nosing) while asking for the behavior. What he wanted was attribution of lineage that lets the reader feel respected without performing gratitude. Andrew + Dylan read as peers who'll notice the credit; they do NOT read as people who want to be thanked.

Final phrasing that worked: *"Building on Andrew's `quality_patterns.md` compounding loop and Dylan's thread on feedback agents..."* Attributes lineage by name + specific artifact; moves forward immediately.

Phrasing that would NOT have worked:
- "Inspired by..." — too warm, reads as performative
- "I loved Andrew's insight about..." — explicit appreciation = brown-nosing
- "Thanks to Andrew and Dylan for..." — direct gratitude performs smallness
- No attribution at all — reads as taking credit

**How to apply:**

1. **When the message is FROM James TO a senior** (especially Andrew, Dylan, Rajat, Jeff): default to subtle lineage attribution. Name the idea + attribute to the originator by their artifact/contribution, not their title or their generosity.
2. **Placement matters.** Homage lands in the opener (sets the lineage), not the close (where it reads as tacking on gratitude).
3. **One mention is enough.** Repeating the credit within the same message inflates it from subtle to performative.
4. **Do not perform humility.** Phrases like "humbly building on" or "grateful to continue" trip the brown-nosing filter even when James asked for warmth.
5. **Test phrase:** if the homage line could be read aloud at Andrew's performance review without anyone cringing, it's subtle enough. If it sounds like a toast, too much.
