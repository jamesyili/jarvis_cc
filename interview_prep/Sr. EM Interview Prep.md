# Management Tactics

1. **Spotlight behavior you want to see.** This has the dual effect of making that person feel rewarded and also serves as a message to everyone else what you value. Targeted Positive Review is a good management strategy.  
2. **“Pinches not punches”, t**hese 3 words capture all the attributes of what good feedback needs to look like \- timely, in small nudges, never breaks the person, is consistent and never surprising. It takes constant work but pinches work better than punches.  
3. **Conflict resolution \- when someone is emotional, don’t participate in that emotion** (unless it’s happiness). “Tell me more.” Stay logical, don’t give into emotions. The goal is to move them from emotional to logical.  
   1. If this is with a report \- I want them to solve their own problems by asking questions. This is how I can scale myself.  
   2. Outside of this, I will need to reflect on the issue further, why did this happen in the first place? What upstream process needs fixing?  
4. **Disconnected / quiet  / soft personalities.**  
   1. Active listening  
      1. What was something that made you happy this past week? What was something that you struggled with?  
      2. Sometimes I play back what I think that person said, just to make sure we’re on the same page. “What I heard you say was …”  
   2. Adapt my communication style. In team meetings, give everyone a chance to speak. Be inclusive “I’d love to get your thoughts on this, even if it’s just a few words”  
   3. Often soft people are very thoughtful and strategic, so I want to show appreciation for them, and leverage their strengths (e.g. putting them on complex issues).  
   4. Build strong trusting relationship over time through consistent and positive interactions. Be patient.  
5. **(When appropriate) Encourage Ninja projects.** Maybe a re-write, maybe just tinkering with some PyTorch notebook. Just see what we can do. These things can either be a waste of time, but they satisfied that itch from those engineers, or they can change the game. Some of my most successful projects came from ninja projects.   
   1. Create anchors rather than sticks in the sand for expectations  
   2. Frame work as a learning challenge  
6. **1:1 time is their time \- focus on the people.** On what they need. On their careers. Not updates unless they bring it up. Start by asking “how are you?”   
   1. Aim for topics that I can’t get outside of 1:1’s  
   2. Backpocket agenda: something from their last performance review, something that happened in the past week that’s related to their area, give them a taste of my problem.   
   3. Ask questions and stop. When people vent \- just listen. Practice active listening. Even if I think I have answers, I don’t necessarily provide them. Instead I help the other person build muscle to problem solve for themselves.  
   4. “And then what happened?” Conversational tones build trust.  
   5. 1:1s are a place to mine for conflict before it turns into a disaster.  
   6. Align strengths to tasks for about 70%  
7. **Adapt my language based on the audience** — fellow managers / directors: use high bandwidth jargon that managers understand, goal is to have an efficient exchange. When it comes to ICs \- speak like a friend, have a conversation. For leadership, it’s to manage upwards, set expectations, and ask for help.  
8. **Sharing \-** Be deliberate about how much you want to share, and when. Balancing transparency with disruptions in a rapidly changing situation.   
9. **Have fun \-** Use offsites liberally, get together, have lunches, discuss non-work items at the beginning of the meeting to relax the atmosphere. Smile and show humility. Be authentic and talk about my hobbies and interests and family outside of work.  
10. **I still code.** The benefits far outweigh the cons. It allows me to  
    1. See how software is made, how an idea gets translated into production, it helps me stay connected and relevant, especially in ML  
    2. It allow some to have high bandwidth conversations with other ICs; to have empathy with pain points / bottlenecks / tooling & efficiency needs.  
    3. Having ownership of a feature keeps me grounded on the importance of small decisions.  
11. **Communicating disagreement**  
    1. Remind me again how you are defining success, and help me see how this proposal works towards that.  
    2. How will we know if this proposal will work?  
    3. What are you confident in in this proposal, and what are you not sure about?  
    4. How important do you think \<perceived weakness\> is? Why?  
    5. It’s not clear to me how this solves for XYZ. Can you explain?  
    6. Have you considered \<alternative idea\>? What did you think?  
12. **Managing High Performers**  
    1. Development:  
       1. Discuss career goals: Ask your employee about their aspirations and help them acquire new skills and experiences to reach those goals.  
       2. Provide exposure: Offer opportunities for them to learn about other parts of the organization and broaden their perspective.  
       3. Develop the entire team: Don't neglect the development of other team members to avoid de-skilling.  
    2. Autonomy:  
       1. Delegate authority: Give your star employee more autonomy and trust them to handle projects and tasks independently.  
       2. Avoid micromanaging: Allow them discretion in how they work and encourage their leadership skills through mentoring opportunities.  
    3. Feedback:  
       1. Acknowledge contributions: Provide positive feedback but avoid excessive praise.  
       2. Encourage self-monitoring: Help your star employee learn to assess their own performance and recognize the contributions of others.  
    4. Workload Management:  
       1. Avoid burnout: Carefully analyze your star employee's workload and remove tasks to prevent overwork.  
       2. Maintain team balance: Ensure the workload is reasonable for everyone on the team to prevent resentment and burnout among other members.  
    5. Group Dynamics:  
       1. Avoid favoritism: Treat all team members fairly and address any tension or jealousy that may arise due to the star employee's performance.  
       2. Encourage communication: Have open conversations with all team members about their motivations and concerns.  
    6. Relationship Building:  
       1. Encourage networking: Help your star employee build relationships with colleagues and learn the value of collaboration.  
       2. Demonstrate the benefits of collaboration: Show them how their work can be enhanced through diverse perspectives.  
    7. Supporting Advancement:  
       1. Don't hoard talent: If your star employee is ready for a bigger challenge, advocate for their promotion within the organization.  
       2. Consider organizational needs: Help them find a suitable position elsewhere in the company to ensure their continued growth and success.

# Meeting Framework

1. A successful meeting is one where once it’s done, it never has to happen again.  
2. First, an agenda to keep everyone on the same page from the start  
   1. Usually it’s one of three things: alignment (aka conflict resolution) or informational (e.g. all hands) or creation / brainstorm  
   2. Someone set the meeting up, so they want something. That’s the agenda. Usually others are the pawns.  
3. Second, having a referee. This person   
   1. manages the flow, keeps us on track, and ensures that everyone has a chance to contribute.   
   2. They also need to know when to let the conversation flow naturally and when to step in, whether that means calling on someone, curbing someone who’s talking too much, or taking detailed conflicts offline to avoid derailing the meeting.   
   3. Importantly, they should have the courage to end the meeting early if we’re not making discernible progress.  
4. Third, almost necessary to have someone, preferably not the referee, taking notes  
5. If I didn’t set the agenda, then I sit and observe who the players are and their incentives. I discern the agenda. Sometimes the ones with the agenda just want someone to synthesize everything into constructive next steps and play it back.  
6. If I’m not the referee, then my role in meetings is to fill the gap  
   1. Be the referee if there isn’t one.  
7. If there’s no note taker, I will ask if someone is taking notes, wait 5-10 seconds, then volunteer to take them if no one offered

## **\[Snap\] Yitong and Stella (Underperformer)**

**Context**

As a Senior Engineering Manager, I faced a complex leadership challenge when Stella, an engineer on the Friend Story Ranking team, was consistently underperforming under her new manager Yitong. The situation was particularly delicate because:

* Yitong was a newly hired Engineering Manager with only two months of experience in the role  
* I was Stella's skip-level manager and had previously managed her directly, so I knew her strengths and areas of improvements  
* Stella's underperformance was impacting team morale and project timelines  
* I needed to address the situation while empowering Yitong to develop his management skills

**Action**

I took a multi-pronged approach to address the situation while supporting both the underperforming employee and the new manager:

Coaching the New Manager:

* Proactively asked Yitong for his thoughts on the team without biasing his point of view. I wanted independent perspectives. Once he also mentioned Stella’s underperformance, I asked him for what he would want to do. I also provided Yitong with a resources at Snap for a structured framework for managing performance issues.  
* Shared insights from my previous experience managing Stella while ensuring Yitong maintained ownership.  
* **Early engagement of HR, even as a heads up:** Once he’s ready, I collaborated with him and HR to develop a tailored Performance Improvement Plan (PIP) for Stella  
  * Involved our HRBP early for guidance on policies and best practices  
  * Ensured thorough documentation of all performance-related interactions

Supporting the Stella:

* Facilitated root cause analysis to understand underlying issues (technical gaps, time management, communication barriers) – Could not unblock herself and needed very detailed guidance. Skill issue, which could be coached. The bigger issue was lack of accountability, and as such she never focused on what she could be doing better – it was always situational or other people.  
* Arranged mentorship with a senior engineer (Xuhui) to provide personalized guidance  
* Suggested resources like technical training and communication coaching

Managing Escalation:

* When Stella escalated her concerns to me, I listened empathetically while reinforcing established expectations  
* Facilitated follow-up meetings between Stella and Yitong to ensure alignment  
* Kept senior leadership informed throughout the process

Results

* **Employee Transition:** Despite support efforts, Stella's performance didn't improve sufficiently. She was offered and accepted an exit package, allowing for a graceful transition  
* **Team Impact:** Addressing the underperformance boosted team morale and productivity, with remaining members feeling reassured about fair performance standards  
* **Leadership Development:** Yitong gained valuable confidence and skills in managing underperforming employees  
* **Process Improvement:** Reinforced the importance of early intervention and clear communication in performance management

Learnings

* **Balance:** The other day I saw a LinkedIn post from her – she’s apparently doing well at her new company, new job,. So I think that low performance can be contextual – it’s about the fit between the person, the situation they’re going through at the time, the company, the team, and what the team needs at the time. As leaders we need to balance empathy and standards to create a fair environment. Goal is not to judge but to support, even in a termination.  
* **Scalable Systems:** Senior leaders must develop systems for proactively monitoring team health and identifying issues early. Effective skip-level intervention requires balancing direct involvement with empowering the direct manager.

## **\[Pinterest\] Raymond and Activation Team Collab**

**Context**

When I just took over leadership of a 13-person team, I inherited a critical collaboration crisis that threatened a marquee 9-month project. The situation involved:

The Project: Revamping Pinterest's new user onboarding experience from topic picker to pin picker, requiring significant backend changes to the home feed relevance system

The Escalation: Raymond, a 9+ year tenure team member who had transitioned to supporting 4 people under me due to family commitments, escalated to my manager and skip about collaboration issues with the Growth Activation team. The Activation team was proposing to launch despite negative MAU and engagement impacts.

The Challenges:

* Raymond was described as "pretty absent" when it came to team leadership  
* The Activation Sr. EM Brian was known to be difficult to work with and lacked ML background  
* Both teams were finger-pointing after 9 months of blocked progress  
* No PM involvement despite the project's strategic importance  
* Limited experiment traffic made iteration extremely difficult

**Action**

I implemented a systematic approach to diagnose and resolve the dysfunction:

Neutral Investigation and Listening Tour:

* Conducted interviews with all stakeholders (Brian, his Sr. Director manager, his reports, Raymond, our tech lead, and my manager) without revealing my intent to step in  
* Deliberately avoided taking positions on the contentious launch proposal, maintaining neutrality by stating I lacked sufficient context  
* Analyzed past collaboration patterns, existing tensions, leadership expectations, and objective project blockers

Root Cause Assessment:

* Identified that my team (especially Raymond) had a "consultant mindset" \- passive involvement, mostly blocking, lacking joint ownership  
* Discovered the Activation team was generating investigations that didn't lead to foundational improvements due to ML knowledge gaps. Excessive motion, but very little actual progress towards the real problem.  
* Recognized the challenges after debugging the user experience and talking to the Tech leads on both sides:   
  * (1) Technical: changing onboarding signals required massive candidate retrieval system changes, and we had suboptimal use of new onboarding signals  
  * (2) Framing: Much of the engagement drops stemmed from low-quality / memey / AI slop content

Strategic Intervention:

* Wrote assessment memo and aligned with my manager before taking action  
* Collaborated with Brian to proposed joint ambitious goal: launch the revamp AND increase MAU by X% (despite revamp potentially being MAU-neutral at best)  
* Secured PM support and implemented governance structure with bi-weekly progress emails and monthly leadership reviews

Hands-On Leadership:

* Co-drove twice-weekly standups with Brian to increase urgency, focused execution, build mutual trust, and develop common technical vocabulary (e.g. do we really need to investigate that now? If we were to resolve this issue, how much do you think this would help us towards the goal of shipping? Created a backlog of eng excellence items while staying focused on the key milestones)  
* Took direct involvement in technical details since Raymond wasn't demonstrating adequate leadership ownership  
* Worked closely with various team members to build trust by being in the trenches so to speak throughout the process

**Results**

Project Success:

* Successfully launched the onboarding revamp at negative engagement but neutral MAU  
* Reframed engagement losses as "non-regrettable" by attributing them primarily to reduced low-quality/memey content  
* Currently on track to hit the ambitious MAU improvement goals

Relationship Transformation:

* Both teams developed strong working relationships and mutual technical understanding  
* Created technical plans with healthy mutual dependencies rather than adversarial dynamics  
* Received applause from leadership across both organizations for the collaboration turnaround

Organizational Decisions:

* The experience provided clear data points that led to transitioning Raymond from management to IC role  
* Established new governance and collaboration patterns for future cross-team projects

**Learnings**

Leadership Presence and Ownership:

* "Showing up as a team is critically important" \- leaders must demonstrate commitment through direct involvement in critical initiatives. Managing cross-team projects requires understanding both technical constraints and organizational dynamics.  
* My actions as a leader signal to the team what is truly important versus what is merely discussed  
* Trust develops "in the trenches" through collaborative problem-solving rather than through formal processes alone. Accountability shows a lot. Building common technical vocabulary across teams with different expertise is essential for effective collaboration

Ownership and Decisiveness in Complex Situations:

* Do or Don’t Do – there’s no “Try”  
* Leaders must be willing to make decisive interventions when dysfunction persists, even when the situation is politically sensitive  
* Neutral investigation followed by clear action is more effective than immediate position-taking. Before doing, get alignment with your leads.

Value Signal Gathering:

* Major collaborative failures often reveal individual performance issues that require separate but related intervention  
* Using high-stakes projects as assessment opportunities can provide valuable data for longer-term organizational decisions

When I first joined the team, I took over Raymond’s entire team of 13 people, and he transitioned to supporting 4 people under me. This was his choice as he had family commitments that prevented him from doing more. Raymond was very nice and available during my onboarding, and he had significant tenure on the team (\> 9 years), so he was very very resourceful. In any case, I received an escalation from Raymond to my manager and skip about some collaboration issues with the Growth Activation team on the marquee projects they’ve been working on for over 9 months – revamping the new user onboarding experience. The goal of the project is to transition from a topic picker onboarding to a pin picker onboarding, so this required a lot of backend changes on the HF relevance side to accommodate. Raymond brought up that the Activation team is going over his head and basically bringing a launch proposal that had a negative 3% impact on MAU and strongly negative engagement. My manager at this time had already given me a few clues (1) Raymond tends to be pretty absent when it comes to the team, (2) the Activation team Sr. EM Brian is not so easy to work with, and they didn’t really come from a ML background, and (3) the staff level engineer working on this project from our side Lingzhi is an underperformer. So I decided to use this opportunity to dig in deep to better assess what’s going on.

I took action without making it seem like I was taking action. I did a listening tour and proactively dug into the past collaboration habits, existing points of tension, leadership expectations from both sides, and how the project is objectively going (what are the blockers and why do we see such losses in the experiment). I side-stepped the launch proposal thing entirely, unless others brought it up proactively. If they did, I just said that I don’t have much context yet. My goal is not to be seen as biased, but rather as a neutral third party coming in to mediate the situation. I talked to Brian, his manager (Sr. Director), his reports on this project, Raymond, Lingzhi, my manager. I then wrote a quick memo with my assessment of the situation and a proposal for the way forward. After she agreed, I then took the following actions.

It turns out that my team members, especially Raymond, lacked accountability and ownership in this collaboration. Very passive involvement, and mostly blocking. Not proactively thinking through the problem and having joint stake in the game. They had a consultant mindset. The Activation team, not really knowing ML, just came up with tons of investigations into specific examples that didn’t really lead to foundational improvements, and they had a lot of pent-up urgency because they had 9 month’s worth of UX changes depending on the backend changes working to launch. Both teams were finger pointing at each other, and the leaders in both orgs were not keen on the details. There was no PM involvement for some reason. 

The objective challenge was also that changing the onboarding signals necessitated a huge change in how we retrieve candidates, especially during the initial homefeed. Improving behind an AB is also extremely painful as we had to manage limited traffic sizes. The engagement drops were mostly a combination of lost on low quality / memey / non-Pinteresty content and real engagement loss due to lower relevance because we weren’t using the new onboarding signal in the right way.

So with help from Lingzhi and Raymond, both of whom I worked closely on this the entire time, I wrote up an objective assessment of what is the gap and why it’s important to overcome for Pinterest as a company. With alignment from Brian and his manager, I established a joint goal between the two teams to launch the revamp AND to bring up new user WAU by 1% which was very ambitious given that the revamp launch may be at best MAU neutral. I engaged their team for PM support and collectively we held a leads review to align on the plan forward, and to have a biweekly email tracking progress along with a monthly leads review where they can unblock us and have clarity on what’s happening. Brian and I co-drove a twice-a-week standup to (1) increase urgency behind both teams, (2) build mutual trust in the trenches, and (3) develop a common technical language so that we’re not just talking past each other. During this process, I was very involved since my manager and I aligned that despite her best coaching, Raymond wasn’t really taking ownership and demonstrating good leadership here.

The result was that we ended up launching the Revamp at negative engagement, and I helped to frame the engagement losses as primarily from low-quality content, which were non-regrettable engagement losses. We were able to also achieve the 1% new user WAU goal with further launches. Both teams actually ended up very close with each other, and developed strong technical plans that had mutual dependence. Leaders from both organizations applauded this change. We’re currently on track to hit the ambitious MAU goals. 

Learning is that showing up as a team is critically important, and trust is very important. Decisiveness as a leader is very important. What you do as a leader sends signals to the rest of the team on what is actually important and what is not.

* Ensure smooth relationship building between a high-performer and new EM  
* Maintain momentum on critical projects during the management transition  
* Set up both individuals for success in their evolving roles

### **Action**

I implemented a strategic approach to support both Jiwen's continued growth and the new manager relationship:

**Maintained Strategic Mentorship:**

* Increased my direct mentoring cadence from bi-weekly to weekly 1:1s with Jiwen, while being careful not to overstep Yitong's boundaries as her direct manager  
* Shifted focus from day-to-day tactical work to career development and strategic progression path  
* Provided strategic context and longer-term vision that complemented her EM's operational guidance

**Expanded Scope and Challenges:**

* Added GraphML scope to Yitong's team portfolio on top of Friend Story Ranking, creating opportunities for Jiwen to stretch into new technical domains  
* Aligned incentives between Jiwen and Yitong by ensuring the expanded scope benefited both their individual goals and team success  
* Positioned new challenges as growth opportunities rather than additional burden

**Facilitated Relationship Building:**

* Included Jiwen in my staff meetings (after coordinating with Yitong) to give her broader organizational exposure and demonstrate confidence in her capabilities  
* Created opportunities for Jiwen and Yitong to build trust through shared accountability for expanded team scope  
* Provided air cover and context to both parties to navigate the new working relationship

**Strategic Resource Allocation:**

* Ensured Yitong had sufficient support and context to effectively manage a high-performer  
* Balanced my involvement to provide career development while empowering the direct management relationship

**Individual Growth:**

* Jiwen successfully established the new GraphML scope, delivering significant impact beyond her original Friend Story Ranking domain  
* Achieved promotion from L5 to L6, becoming one of the few female L6 engineers in our organization

* GraphML scope benefited both Friend Stories and expanded impact outside the original team boundaries  
* Smooth management transition with strong working relationship between Jiwen and Yitong  
* Maintained technical momentum while successfully scaling organizational structure

* Created a model for developing high-potential individual contributors during rapid team scaling

### **Learnings**

**Investment in High Performers:**

* "Spend lots of time with strong performers" \- top talent requires and deserves significant leadership investment to reach their potential  
* High performers thrive when paired with challenges that stretch their capabilities rather than being constrained by current scope

**Support Rather Than Limitation:**

* "Don't limit them but instead give them more support" \- removing barriers and providing resources is more effective than trying to contain high performers within existing boundaries  
* Strategic scope expansion can align individual growth with organizational needs

**Managing Through Transitions:**

* Skip-level relationships can be maintained and leveraged during management transitions if boundaries are clearly established and communicated  
* New manager success requires both autonomy and strategic support from senior leadership

**Organizational Design:**

* Rapid scaling requires thoughtful consideration of how to maintain development opportunities for existing talent while introducing new leadership layers  
* Incentive alignment between managers and high-performing reports is crucial for successful team dynamics

**Diversity and Inclusion:**

* Creating pathways for underrepresented groups to advance requires intentional mentorship and strategic opportunity creation  
* Success stories can create positive momentum for broader organizational diversity goals

2. ### Discover Tile / Content Quality at Snap

   1. Context:  
      1. **Platform Risk:** Our platform was grappling with a severe issue of sensitive content in the feed, posing a substantial risk to our brand reputation and user trust. The persistent issue threatens the integrity of our platform, with legal and regulation ramifications.  
      2. **Engagement vs. Polarization:** This content, though it drove high engagement (e.g., increased view time, taps, and sends), was extremely polarizing, leading to a significant number of hides and reports.  
      3. **Previous Failures:** Repeated attempts to address the problem had not only failed but also resulted in a loss of user engagement and non-engagement from (XFN) teams.  
      4. **Urgent Resolution Needed:** As I was taking over the team, it became clear that a successful solution was essential for the growth and sustainability of our content ecosystem.  
   2. Approach 1: Steps  
      1. **Set Success Criteria Metrics:** Define the objective metrics to showcase progress and measure success. Also allows us to throw things up on a dashboard to align everyone.  
      2. **Align / Engage XFN Teams, Leadership:** Collaborate with policy, moderation, legal, and other engineering functions to make the initiative successful. There are often unspoken reasons why such efforts were not successful before.  
      3. **Investigations and Finding the Bottlenecks in our systems:** We need truth seeking behavior and really investigate and debug to understand why such content is surfaced so much. Drive multiple investigations and SEV post mortems to improve automation, human moderation quality, and policy refinements. Start with the data, user reports, etc.  
      4. **Technical Foundations:** We need to make sure there are both long term and short term technical investments, so that we can start with small wins as well as drive a long term solution to the user problem.  
      5. **Faster Experimentation / Feedback loop:** Decide on the treatment of identified content (e.g., special treatment, complete removal) based on user engagement data.  
   3. Approach 2: Actions  
      1. **Collaborate and Set Joint OKRs:**  
         1. Worked with the Operations Director and Trust and Safety Sr. Engineering Managers to rally the XFN group into swift action.  
         2. Set joint OKRs to establish clear accountability and guardrails for each function, and identify the necessary resources for success.  
      2. **Stakeholder Management:**  
         1. The Operations Director was initially hesitant, fearing blame for the quality of human labeling for sensitive content. I actively listened to her concerns and empathized with her position. I reframed the conversation, highlighting the need for monitoring and continuous improvement in labeling quality rather than assigning blame. I emphasized the role of external contracting companies in the labeling process, suggesting we focus on strengthening their accountability. This shift in perspective helped alleviate her concerns and fostered a collaborative relationship.  
         2. The Policy Manager was also worried, since this content was technically not violative. He worried that we would risk regulation and questions that this is not fair to the creators of this content. We worked closely to come up with a new framework (borderline content) around creator incentives and the need to disincentivize content that searches the boundary of the policy line to gamify distribution.  
      3. **Technical Deep-Dives and Strategy Setting:**  
         1. Held several technical deep-dives with the team to analyze past efforts and identify changes needed.  
         2. Led multiple investigations and SEV post mortems related to content quality and moderation.  
            1. Used data to identify that a small group of users were responsible for the majority of engagement with sensitive content.  
            2. Implemented strong demotions for users outside this group, while applying smaller demotions for users within the group. Ensured content was not completely removed from any user’s feed as it was not policy-violating.  
         3. Developed processes and accountability for different parts of the ambiguous problem and gathered learnings about the problem space.  
      4. **Investment in Content Understanding:**  
         1. Delegate to tech leads to build computer vision classifiers to detect suggestive content. Analyze user interactions to determine how to identify sensitive content using engagement or visual signals.  
         2. Made strategic investments in the Content Understanding team to build a suggestive ML classifier for images and videos.  
         3. Developed CLIP embeddings and ANN, and a creator reward & strike system, necessary for managing the perception of new and lightly engaged users.  
      5. **Faster Experimentation / Feedback Loop:**  
         1. Set the first strategic milestone to reduce user reports by quickly building the pHide and pReport heads in the L2 model, which helped build momentum through repeated launches.  
         2. Established an oncall loop leading to meaningful discussions and concrete improvements in automation, human moderation quality oversight, and policy refinements.  
   4. Results  
      1. Achieved a sustained 60+% drop in user reports on Discover through 27 ranking treatments  across two years, addressing the content quality problem.  
      2. Improved the perception of Feed Tile Quality, as evidenced by a 35% drop in the prevalence of suggestive and sensitive content overall, and this topic no longer being the worst customer pain point in user surveys.  
      3. This effort was recognized at two separate company-wide Engineering Q\&A sessions and featured on the company-wide Snappy portal.The technical foundations built by this work continue to be leveraged to tackle content quality issues.  
      4. Built a lot of trust with the Operations team and its key leaders.  
      5. Learning  
         1. Building trust with stakeholders, especially those with concerns about potential blame, is crucial for successful collaboration.  
         2. Active listening, empathy, and reframing conversations can help address concerns and foster a shared sense of ownership.  
         3. Data-driven decision-making and a focus on continuous improvement can build confidence and ensure sustainable solutions.
