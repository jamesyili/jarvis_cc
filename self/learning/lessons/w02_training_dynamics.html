Training Dynamics

You know what one training step is (Lesson 01, June: forward → loss → backward → step). This lesson is about what a hundred thousand of them do — why the knobs exist, how runs die, and how to read a loss curve the way a clinician reads an ECG.

What this lesson changes: after ~60–75 min you can (1) explain what each of the four training defenses — warmup, decay, clipping, weight decay — protects against, mechanically; (2) diagnose the six canonical loss-curve pathologies on sight; (3) run the sanity ladder (init-loss check → overfit-one-batch → scale) on any run your team shows you; and (4) interrogate the training half of an HF fine-tuning review with the same authority you bring to a two-tower design. §2Why Adam, and the four defenses

SGD updates every parameter with the same global step size. A transformer (and even more so a recsys model) is the worst possible customer for that: gradient magnitudes differ by orders of magnitude across layers, and — the part you'll recognize — embedding rows are sparse customers: any given item's row gets a gradient only on steps where the item appears. Adam fixes both with two running estimates per parameter:

m ← β₁·m + (1−β₁)·g # first moment: smoothed gradient (direction) v ← β₂·v + (1−β₂)·g² # second moment: smoothed magnitude θ ← θ − lr · m̂ / (√v̂ + ε) # per-parameter step: big for quiet params, damped for loud ones

Dividing by √v normalizes each parameter's step by its own typical gradient scale. A rarely-updated embedding row (small accumulated v) takes confident steps when its item finally shows up; a hot dense layer (large v) is damped. AdamW is the same thing with weight decay applied directly to the weights rather than mixed into the gradient — decay strength stops depending on gradient scale, which is why it's the default.

Bridge: this is the same reason your feature-embedding infra ended up with per-feature adaptive optimizers (Adagrad lineage) — power-law item frequency makes any single global step size wrong for both the head and the tail simultaneously. Adam is that lesson, generalized. Where it breaks: Adam's state costs 2 extra values per parameter — for a 10⁸-row embedding table that's real memory, which is why big recsys systems often run different optimizers for embeddings vs dense layers. Worth asking which choice your team's fine-tuning stack made.

The four defenses, by what kills the run without them

DefenseThe death it preventsMechanism
LR warmupDivergence in the first few hundred stepsAt step 10, v is an estimate from ~10 samples — garbage. Full LR ÷ garbage variance = huge, wrongly-scaled steps that can break training before statistics stabilize. Warmup keeps steps small until v means something.
LR decay (cosine)Endless bouncing around the minimumLate in training the loss surface's remaining structure is finer than the step size; annealing lets the model settle instead of orbit. (Shape matters less than people argue; having decay matters.)
Gradient clippingOne bad batch nuking the weightsCap the global gradient norm (typically 1.0). A rare pathological batch produces a 100× gradient once — clipped, it's a non-event; unclipped, it's a loss spike the run may never recover from.
Weight decaySlow drift into overfit/bloated weightsConstant pull toward zero — capacity control. In fine-tuning reviews the question is whether it's on for embeddings too (often it shouldn't be).

§4The sanity ladder

The debugging recipe, in the order that isolates cause. Each rung is cheap and each failure localizes the bug:

1. Loss at init ≈ ln(vocab)? # untrained model should be exactly as wrong as chance GPT-2 vocab 50,257 → expect ≈ 10.8. Wildly off → bug in loss/labels/init, not "needs training". 2. Overfit ONE batch to ~0 loss. # can the model+loss+optimizer memorize 32 examples? Can't → wiring bug (masking, off-by-one labels, LR). No amount of data fixes wiring. 3. Overfit 1% of data. # does val loss behave sanely on a toy split? 4. Full run, watch the FIRST 500 steps against the clinic patterns. 5. Only now discuss capacity, data, and hyperparameters.
Recsys instantiation of rung 1: a sampled-softmax next-item model with k negatives should start at ≈ ln(k+1) — with 511 negatives, ≈ 6.24. A CLR-style in-batch-softmax with batch 4,096 starts ≈ ln(4096) ≈ 8.3. If your team's run starts at 2.1, the labels are leaking or the negatives are broken; the expected init loss is a number you can compute before the run exists, and asking for it is the fastest credibility move in a training review.
Self-explain
Why does "can't overfit one batch" rule out data quantity as the problem? One sentence.
Memorizing 32 fixed examples requires no generalization — only that gradients flow from a correctly-computed loss to the right parameters; failure means the mechanism is broken (masking, label alignment, optimizer state), and mechanisms don't heal with more data.

§5The softmax bottleneck — recsys training's defining constraint

Language models compute the full softmax: vocabulary ~50K, final layer cost per token = 2·d·V FLOPs — fine. Your catalog is 10⁸–10⁹. The full softmax is architecturally impossible, and every recsys training recipe is a workaround wearing a different name:

WorkaroundMechanismThe bias it introducesThe fix
Sampled softmaxScore the positive against k sampled negatives instead of all VDepends on the sampling distribution q — the gradient is biased unless correctedSubtract log q(item) from each logit (the classic correction)
In-batch negativesOther positives in the batch are your negatives — free, since their embeddings are already computedq = popularity (items appear as negatives proportional to engagement) → popular items over-punished, tail under-trainedLogQ correction: same logit correction with q̂ = estimated in-batch frequency
Two-tower + ANNDon't decode at all — dot-product against a prebuilt indexNot a softmax at serve time at all; training still uses one of the above— (this is your home turf)
This is CLR's training loop. The in-batch-negatives + LogQ pattern is (a lineage of) how sampling-bias-corrected two-tower training works, and it's the direct ancestor of how CLR trains today. The reason this section is in a transformer lesson: a GR-SID decoder faces the same bottleneck in a new costume — TIGER's answer is that decoding over 3×256 codes replaces the 10⁸-way softmax with three 256-way softmaxes. That's the honest efficiency argument for semantic IDs, and it's also why codebook sizing is a serving decision, not just a quality one. When ATG or anyone shows you a generative trainer, your first training question is now: what plays the role of the negative sampler here, and what's the correction?

Why √v and not v — one paragraph

Dividing by v (the variance) would make the step size scale like 1/g — big gradients would produce tiny steps and the update would lose all magnitude information. Dividing by √v (the standard deviation) makes steps approximately scale-free: a parameter whose gradient is consistently 10× larger takes the same effective step as one 10× smaller, so the learning rate means the same thing everywhere in the network. That normalization is the entire point; ε just prevents division blow-ups for near-silent parameters.

Warmup, precisely: why the first steps are dangerous

With β₂ = 0.999, v has an effective memory of ~1,000 steps; at step 20 it's an average of 20 noisy samples, and bias-correction amplifies rather than fixes the noise (it corrects the mean, not the variance of the estimate). A parameter that happened to see small gradients gets a huge 1/√v̂ multiplier and a violent step. At small scale the loss surface forgives this; at large scale (or with long sequences and hot embeddings) it doesn't. Warmup is not a superstition; it's holding fire until the estimator earns trust.
